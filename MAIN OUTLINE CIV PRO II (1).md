# **Week 1 Outline: Course Introduction & Pleading the Complaint** {#week-1-outline:-course-introduction-&-pleading-the-complaint}

## **I. Course Overview** {#i.-course-overview}

### **A. Course Content Progression** {#a.-course-content-progression}

Complaint → Answer/Motion to Dismiss → Discovery → Summary Judgment → Trial → Post-Trial Motions → Appeal → Claim/Issue Preclusion

### **B. Civil Case Timeline** {#b.-civil-case-timeline}

Complaint → \[Default OR Answer OR Motion to Dismiss\] → Discovery → Summary Judgment → Trial → Appeal → Supreme Court

     ↓                    ↓

  Rule 8(a)          Rule 55 (default)

                     Rule 8(b)-(c) (answer)

                     Rule 12(b)(6) (motion to dismiss)

### **C. Two Court Systems** {#c.-two-court-systems}

* **Federal Courts**: District Courts (94) → Circuit Courts of Appeals (13) → Supreme Court  
* **State Courts**: Trial Courts → Intermediate Appellate Courts (most states) → State Supreme Court  
* **Volume**: 98% civil cases in state court; 2% in federal court

---

## **II. What Are Pleadings?** {#ii.-what-are-pleadings?}

### **A. Definition** {#a.-definition}

Parties' initial statements of their cases filed *before* full discovery—parties don't have complete facts when pleading.

### **B. Types of Pleadings** {#b.-types-of-pleadings}

| Pleading | Filed By | Purpose | Key Rules |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Complaint** | Plaintiff | Alleges claims & requests remedies | Rule 8(a) |
| **Answer** | Defendant | Responds to allegations; asserts defenses/counterclaims | Rules 8(b)-(c), 13 |

### **C. Historical Purposes of Pleading (Evolution)** {#c.-historical-purposes-of-pleading-(evolution)}

1. **Common Law**: Narrow issues to single dispute (hypertechnical, no second chances)  
2. **Code Pleading**: State "facts" showing cause of action—not evidence, not conclusions (still used in some states)  
3. **Notice Pleading** (1938 Federal Rules): Give defendant notice of claims (plaintiff-protective)  
4. **Plausibility Pleading** (2007–2009): Must plead facts making claim plausible (defendant-protective)

---

## **III. Plaintiff's Complaint Requirements** {#iii.-plaintiff's-complaint-requirements}

### **A. Rule 8(a) — Three Required Elements** {#a.-rule-8(a)-—-three-required-elements}

1. **8(a)(1)**: Short and plain statement of grounds for jurisdiction  
2. **8(a)(2)**: Short and plain statement of claim showing entitlement to relief ← *This is where pleading standards apply*  
3. **8(a)(3)**: Demand for relief (prayer for judgment)

### **B. Rule 8(d) — Pleading Flexibility** {#b.-rule-8(d)-—-pleading-flexibility}

* **8(d)(1)**: Simple, concise, direct allegations; no technical forms required  
* **8(d)(2)**: Alternative/hypothetical claims permitted  
* **8(d)(3)**: Inconsistent claims permitted (e.g., "contract invalid" AND "breach of valid contract")

### **C. Rule 8(e) — Construction** {#c.-rule-8(e)-—-construction}

Pleadings "must be construed so as to do justice"

### **D. Rule 10(b) — Form of Pleadings** {#d.-rule-10(b)-—-form-of-pleadings}

* Numbered paragraphs  
* Each paragraph limited to single set of circumstances  
* Separate counts for separate claims when clarity requires

---

## **IV. Evolution of Pleading Standards Under Rule 8(a)(2)** {#iv.-evolution-of-pleading-standards-under-rule-8(a)(2)}

### **A. Notice Pleading Era (1938–2007)** {#a.-notice-pleading-era-(1938–2007)}

**Standard**: *Conley v. Gibson* (1957) — Complaint sufficient unless "it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts in support of his claim which would entitle him to relief."

**Key Case**: *Dioguardi v. Durning* (2d Cir. 1944\)

* **Facts**: Pro se plaintiff filed barely comprehensible complaint about customs collector losing/selling his imported tonics  
* **Holding**: Complaint sufficient—court could identify claims (conversion, violation of auction regulations) even though plaintiff didn't cite specific legal theories  
* **Principle**: Chief purpose of pleading is to give *notice*; court identifies applicable law from alleged facts  
* **Outcome**: Dioguardi lost at trial but won the pleading principle—12(b)(6) asks whether plaintiff *could* be entitled to relief if facts are true, not whether plaintiff will ultimately win

**Characteristics of Notice Pleading**:

* Low threshold for sufficiency  
* Court takes all factual allegations as true  
* Court identifies applicable legal theories even if not pleaded  
* Screening function shifted to discovery and summary judgment

### **B. Plausibility Pleading Era (2007–Present)** {#b.-plausibility-pleading-era-(2007–present)}

### **1\. *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* (2007)** {#1.-bell-atlantic-corp.-v.-twombly-(2007)}

* **Context**: Antitrust claim alleging conspiracy among telecom companies  
* **Facts Alleged**: Parallel conduct among competitors  
* **Problem**: Parallel conduct consistent with both (a) illegal conspiracy AND (b) lawful independent business decisions  
* **Holding**: Conley's "no set of facts" standard "has earned its retirement"  
* **New Standard**: Must plead "enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face"—must "nudge" claims "across the line from conceivable to plausible"  
* **Policy Driver**: Discovery costs; need screening mechanism earlier than summary judgment

### **2\. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (2009)** {#2.-ashcroft-v.-iqbal-(2009)}

* **Context**: Civil rights claim against AG Ashcroft and FBI Director Mueller for discriminatory detention post-9/11  
* **Question**: Does Twombly apply to all civil cases or just antitrust?  
* **Answer**: Applies to ALL civil actions—based on Rule 8, which governs all civil cases

---

## **V. The Twombly-Iqbal Two-Step Analysis** {#v.-the-twombly-iqbal-two-step-analysis}

### **Step 1: Identify and Discard Conclusory Allegations** {#step-1:-identify-and-discard-conclusory-allegations}

**Rule**: Court accepts as true only "well-pleaded" factual allegations—NOT:

* Legal conclusions  
* "Threadbare recitals of elements of a cause of action"  
* "Formulaic recitation of the elements"

**Iqbal Example — Deemed Conclusory**:

* Defendants "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed to subject" plaintiff to harsh conditions "solely on account of his religion, race, and/or national origin"  
* Ashcroft was "principal architect" of policy  
* Mueller was "instrumental" in adoption

*Why conclusory?* These restate elements of a discrimination claim without providing underlying facts.

### **Step 2: Assess Whether Remaining Facts Plausibly State a Claim** {#step-2:-assess-whether-remaining-facts-plausibly-state-a-claim}

**Rule**: After stripping conclusions, determine whether remaining factual allegations "plausibly give rise to an entitlement to relief"

**The Competing Inferences Problem**: When facts equally support innocent and guilty explanations, plaintiff must plead something more to make liability inference *more plausible* than innocent explanation.

**Iqbal Example — Remaining Facts**:

* FBI arrested/detained thousands of Arab Muslim men  
* Ashcroft and Mueller approved policy of holding post-9/11 detainees in restrictive conditions until cleared

**Two Competing Inferences**:

| Guilty Inference | Innocent Inference |
| ----- | ----- |
| Targeted Arab Muslims because of animus toward race/religion | Targeted Arab Muslims because 9/11 attackers were Arab Muslims—legitimate national security response |

**Result**: Innocent explanation was at least as plausible → claim fails

### **Summary: Plausibility Standard** {#summary:-plausibility-standard}

* More than "possible" or "conceivable"  
* Less than "probable"  
* Context-specific inquiry using "judicial experience and common sense"  
* When facts equally support competing inferences, plaintiff loses

---

## **VI. Rule 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss** {#vi.-rule-12(b)(6)-motion-to-dismiss}

### **A. Purpose** {#a.-purpose}

Attack legal sufficiency of complaint—"So what?" motion

### **B. When Used** {#b.-when-used}

1. **Not Legally Sufficient**: No recognized legal claim (e.g., suing for not attending party after RSVP)  
2. **Not Factually Sufficient**: Allegations don't meet plausibility standard

### **C. Key Procedural Points** {#c.-key-procedural-points}

* Court assumes all factual allegations TRUE for purposes of motion  
* Court does NOT weigh evidence or resolve factual disputes  
* If court considers matters outside pleadings → converts to Rule 56 summary judgment motion (Rule 12(d))  
* Dismissal usually with leave to amend (opportunity to replead)

### **D. What 12(b)(6) Does NOT Do** {#d.-what-12(b)(6)-does-not-do}

* Does not decide whether plaintiff will win  
* Does not resolve factual disputes  
* Does not require plaintiff to prove case

---

## **VII. Rule 9(b) — Heightened Pleading for Fraud** {#vii.-rule-9(b)-—-heightened-pleading-for-fraud}

### **A. The Rule** {#a.-the-rule}

"In alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake. Malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of a person's mind may be alleged generally."

### **B. Requirements** {#b.-requirements}

Must allege with specificity: **when**, **where**, and **how** fraud was committed

### **C. Rationales (and Their Weaknesses)** {#c.-rationales-(and-their-weaknesses)}

1. **Fair Notice**: Generic fraud allegation doesn't specify which acts were fraudulent *(but same true for negligence)*  
2. **Protecting Reputation**: Fraud claims damage defendant's reputation *(but so do discrimination claims)*  
3. **Reducing Frivolous Litigation**: Fraud easy to allege, hard to prove *(but same true for many claims)*

### **D. Limited to Rule 9(b) Categories** {#d.-limited-to-rule-9(b)-categories}

*Leatherman v. Tarrant County* (1993): Courts cannot extend heightened pleading to categories not listed in Rule 9(b)—that requires rule amendment, not judicial interpretation.

---

## **VIII. Plaintiff's Pleading Checklist** {#viii.-plaintiff's-pleading-checklist}

1. ☐ Jurisdictional statement — Rule 8(a)(1)  
2. ☐ Short and plain statement of claims (plausible facts, not just conclusions) — Rule 8(a)(2)  
3. ☐ Demand for relief — Rule 8(a)(3)  
4. ☐ Numbered paragraphs — Rule 10(b)  
5. ☐ Separate counts if multiple claims — Rule 10(b)  
6. ☐ If alleging fraud: particularity requirements — Rule 9(b)  
7. ☐ Care and candor requirements — Rule 11 *(covered Week 2\)*

---

## **IX. Key Distinctions** {#ix.-key-distinctions}

| Notice Pleading | Plausibility Pleading |
| ----- | ----- |
| Conley (1957) | Twombly/Iqbal (2007/2009) |
| "No set of facts" that could support claim | Facts must make claim "plausible on its face" |
| Plaintiff-protective | Defendant-protective |
| Low bar to survive 12(b)(6) | Higher bar—conclusions stripped, then plausibility assessed |
| Screening at discovery/SJ stage | Earlier screening at pleading stage |

---

## **X. Policy Implications of Plausibility Standard** {#x.-policy-implications-of-plausibility-standard}

**Favors Defendants** (especially government, large corporations):

* Dismissal before expensive discovery  
* Information asymmetry: defendants control facts plaintiff needs to plead plausibly

**Disadvantages Plaintiffs** (especially individuals):

* Catch-22: Need facts to plead plausibly, but facts are in defendant's control and only obtainable through discovery  
* Higher barrier to courtroom access

# **Week 2 Civil Procedure Outline** {#week-2-civil-procedure-outline}

## **I. MOTION TO DISMISS UNDER RULE 12(b)(6)** {#i.-motion-to-dismiss-under-rule-12(b)(6)}

### **A. Purpose and Standard** {#a.-purpose-and-standard}

* **Motion**: "MOVE to dismiss" (not "Motion to dismiss")  
* **Standard**: Assuming all well-pleaded allegations are true, plaintiff still loses as a matter of law  
* **Also called**: The "So what?" motion  
* **Timing**: Occurs before discovery begins

### **B. When Used** {#b.-when-used-1}

1. **Not legally sufficient**: Plaintiff doesn't state a claim the law recognizes  
   * Example: Failure to attend party after RSVP  
   * Example: Employee suing employer for negligence when only workers' compensation available  
2. **Not factually sufficient**: Complaint fails plausibility pleading under *Twombly-Iqbal*

### **C. When NOT Used** {#c.-when-not-used}

* **Cannot dispute facts** at this stage  
* If defendant presents evidence outside the complaint AND court considers it → motion converts to summary judgment motion under FRCP 12(d)

---

## **II. PLEADING STANDARDS: NOTICE vs. PLAUSIBILITY** {#ii.-pleading-standards:-notice-vs.-plausibility}

### **A. Notice Pleading (Traditional/Ohio)** {#a.-notice-pleading-(traditional/ohio)}

* **Current in Ohio**: Still uses notice pleading standard  
* **Requirement**: Short and plain statement showing entitlement to relief  
* **Purpose**: Give defendant fair notice of claims

### **B. Plausibility Pleading (*Twombly-Iqbal* \- Federal)** {#b.-plausibility-pleading-(twombly-iqbal---federal)}

### **1\. The Two-Step Analysis** {#1.-the-two-step-analysis}

**Step 1: Strip Out Conclusory Allegations**

* Identify "threadbare recitals of the elements" of a cause of action  
* These get NO presumption of truth  
* Example: "Defendants knowingly and willfully discriminated against plaintiff"  
  * Just restates legal standard  
  * Could be copy-pasted into any discrimination complaint  
* **Key**: Just repeating elements of a claim is NOT enough

**Step 2: Assess Remaining Facts for Plausibility**

* Take remaining well-pleaded facts as true  
* Ask: Do they *plausibly* suggest entitlement to relief?  
* Must be more than "possible" or "conceivable"  
* Must cross the line to "plausible"

### **2\. What is a "Well-Pleaded Allegation"?** {#2.-what-is-a-"well-pleaded-allegation"?}

* **An allegation that is NOT conclusory**  
* Contains specific facts, not just legal conclusions

## **III. SWANSON v. CITIBANK \- APPLYING TWOMBLY-IQBAL** {#iii.-swanson-v.-citibank---applying-twombly-iqbal}

### **A. Case Overview** {#a.-case-overview}

* **Court**: 7th Circuit (2010)  
* **Claims**: Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3605\) and Equal Credit Opportunity Act  
* **Procedure**: District court dismissed under 12(b)(6); plaintiff appealed

### **B. The Split: What Does "Plausibility" Require?** {#b.-the-split:-what-does-"plausibility"-require?}

| Issue | Majority (Judge Wood) | Posner's Dissent |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **What plausibility requires** | "Could it have happened?" — tells coherent story | Must be more likely than innocent explanation |
| **Level of factual detail** | Type \+ who \+ when \= sufficient | Need facts ruling out obvious innocent explanations |
| **Role of competing inferences** | Don't stack inferences side-by-side at pleading stage | If innocent explanation is more obvious, complaint fails |
| **Whose experience matters** | — | Judge's "common sense" about appraisal errors |

### **C. Majority View (Judge Wood)** {#c.-majority-view-(judge-wood)}

* **Plausibility ≠ Probability**  
* Court should NOT decide "whose version to believe" or "which version is more likely"  
* **Test**: "Give enough details about the subject-matter to present a story that holds together"  
* Ask: "**Could** these things have happened" (not "**did** they happen")  
* For straightforward cases: Don't need to stack competing inferences  
* Swanson identified: type of discrimination \+ by whom \+ when \= **sufficient**

### **D. Posner's Dissent (Strict *Iqbal* Reading)** {#d.-posner's-dissent-(strict-iqbal-reading)}

* Requires dismissal when there's an "obvious alternative explanation" more plausible than liability  
* Appraisal error is common; discrimination is less likely  
* Under this view, Swanson should lose

### **E. Key Insight** {#e.-key-insight}

* **"Iqbal requires largely unconstrained comparative judgments by judges that depend on a judge's background knowledge and assumptions"**  
* Same facts, same standard → opposite conclusions based on judge's view of what's "plausible"

---

## **IV. EXAM STRATEGY: TWO-LAYER ANALYSIS** {#iv.-exam-strategy:-two-layer-analysis}

### **A. Layer 1: *Twombly-Iqbal* "On Its Face"** {#a.-layer-1:-twombly-iqbal-"on-its-face"}

**The Two-Step Test:**

1. Strip out conclusory allegations  
   * Mere "threadbare recitals of elements"  
   * Legal conclusions don't get assumption of truth  
2. Assess remaining facts for plausibility  
   * Do well-pleaded facts "nudge" claim from conceivable to plausible?  
   * When facts equally support innocent and guilty inferences → plaintiff loses

**Key Language:**

* Courts draw on "judicial experience and common sense"  
* Must show more than "sheer possibility" of misconduct

### **B. Layer 2: *Swanson's* Softening** {#b.-layer-2:-swanson's-softening}

**Acknowledge the more permissive reading:**

* Plausibility ≠ probability  
* Ask "could this have happened," not "did it happen"  
* In straightforward cases, don't stack competing inferences  
* A "story that holds together" suffices

**The Tension:**

* Posner's dissent shows strict *Iqbal* reading would dismiss *Swanson*  
* Majority declined to go that far

### **C. Exam Answer Structure** {#c.-exam-answer-structure}

"Under Twombly-Iqbal, \[apply strict two-step test\]. However, courts like the Seventh Circuit in Swanson have interpreted plausibility more permissively, holding that \[apply softer standard\]. Here, \[argue both ways\]."

**Shows doctrinal sophistication**

### **A. If You Are the PLAINTIFF** {#a.-if-you-are-the-plaintiff}

* **ERR ON THE SIDE OF "FACT" PLEADING**  
* Allege specific facts about each element  
* Example structure:

* Move to dismiss under 12(b)(6) much more often  
* Target conclusory allegations  
* Highlight when facts support multiple inferences (including innocent ones)

### **C. Catch-22 Problem** {#c.-catch-22-problem}

* Plaintiffs need facts that only discovery can reveal  
* But must plead those facts to survive motion to dismiss  
* Discovery doesn't begin until after motion to dismiss is resolved

## **VIII. RULE 11(b) \- REPRESENTATIONS/CERTIFICATIONS** {#viii.-rule-11(b)---representations/certifications}

**By "presenting" a document through:**

* Signing

### **B. What You Certify (to best of knowledge, after reasonable inquiry):** {#b.-what-you-certify-(to-best-of-knowledge,-after-reasonable-inquiry):}

### **(1) No Improper Purpose** {#(1)-no-improper-purpose}

* Not presented for improper purpose, such as:  
  * Harassment  
  * Delay

### **(2) Legal Contentions Are Warranted \- RULE 11(b)(2)** {#(2)-legal-contentions-are-warranted---rule-11(b)(2)}

**Claims/defenses/legal contentions are:**

* Warranted by existing law, OR  
* Nonfrivolous argument for:

* Factual contentions have evidentiary support, OR

* Denials of factual contentions are warranted on the evidence, OR  
* If specifically identified: reasonably based on belief or lack of information

## **IX. HAYS v. SONY CORP. \- RULE 11 VIOLATIONS** {#ix.-hays-v.-sony-corp.---rule-11-violations}

### **A. Facts** {#a.-facts}

* **Plaintiffs**: Stephanie Hays and Gail MacDonald (high school teachers, Des Plaines, IL)  
* **What happened**: Wrote manual for DEC word processors  
  * School bought Sony word processors  
  * School gave Sony plaintiffs' manual  
  * Sony adapted it for free  
  * Sony never sold or distributed adapted manual

### **B. Claims Filed** {#b.-claims-filed}

1. **Count I**: Common law copyright infringement  
2. **Count II**: Statutory (federal) copyright infringement

**Remedies sought:**

* Compensatory damages  
* Punitive damages  
* Accounting for profits  
* Injunction  
* Attorney's fees

### **C. Rule 11 Problems Identified** {#c.-rule-11-problems-identified}

### **Problem \#1: Legal Basis \- Rule 11(b)(2)** {#problem-#1:-legal-basis---rule-11(b)(2)}

**Common law copyright claim was FRIVOLOUS**

* Copyright Act of 1976 **explicitly abolished** common law copyright as of January 1, 1978  
* Well before events in this case  
* Most basic research would have revealed this  
* Attorney Guyon had NO legal basis for Count I

### **Problem \#2: Evidentiary Support \- Rule 11(b)(3)** {#problem-#2:-evidentiary-support---rule-11(b)(3)}

| Remedy Sought | Why It Failed |
| ----- | ----- |
| Statutory damages/attorney's fees | No copyright registration within 3 months of publication (required by 17 U.S.C. § 412\) |
| Accounting for profits | Sony's affidavit showed zero sales \= zero revenue \= zero profits |
| Actual damages | No evidence plaintiffs planned to publish or lost any market |
| Punitive damages | NOT available in federal copyright suits |

**Only potentially valid claim**: Injunction for statutory copyright infringement

* But Guyon never focused on this  
* Kept pressing frivolous claims

### **Problem \#3: Inadequate Pre-Filing Inquiry** {#problem-#3:-inadequate-pre-filing-inquiry}

**Guyon did essentially NO investigation:**

* Never researched whether common law copyright still existed (it didn't)  
* Never contacted Sony to ask if it was selling/distributing manual  
* Never developed evidence of lost profits or marketing plans  
* **His excuse**: "Didn't know how to find out whether Sony was selling"  
  * Court: Could have written a letter asking\!

### **D. The Rule 11 Standard Articulated (Judge Posner)** {#d.-the-rule-11-standard-articulated-(judge-posner)}

"Rule 11 demands 'an objective determination of whether a sanctioned party's conduct was reasonable under the circumstances.' In effect it imposes a negligence standard, for negligence is a failure to use reasonable care."

### **Critical Points:** {#critical-points:}

1. **Objective Standard**  
   * Good faith is NO defense  
   * "Ignorance is no defense"  
2. **No Locality Rule**  
   * Unlike medical malpractice  
   * National standard applies  
3. **Non-Specialists Must Compensate**  
   * If you lack expertise, you must EITHER:  
     * Associate with a lawyer who has it, OR  
     * "Bone up on the relevant law at every step"  
     * "Litigate very carefully, just as a new driver must drive very carefully"  
4. **Cost-Benefit Analysis** (*Szabo Food Service*)  
   * "Amount of investigation required depends on:  
     * Time available to investigate  
     * Probability that more investigation will turn up important evidence"  
   * Rule does NOT require steps that are not cost-justified

### **E. The Sanction** {#e.-the-sanction}

* **Amount**: $14,895.46 against attorney Guyon  
* **NOT** against plaintiffs themselves  
* **Calculation** (graduated percentages of Sony's attorney's fees):  
  * 10% of fees before April 1, 1986 (when hopelessness should have been obvious)  
  * 50% of fees after April 1  
  * 75% of fees on futile last-minute filing

## **X. HUNTER v. EARTHGRAINS \- ARGUING FOR CHANGE IN LAW** {#x.-hunter-v.-earthgrains---arguing-for-change-in-law}

### **A. Facts & Procedure** {#a.-facts-&-procedure}

* **Plaintiff**: Pamela Hunter (attorney) filed class action  
* **Defendants**: Earthgrains bakery  
* **Claims**: Title VII employment discrimination (minority employees after bakery closed)  
* **Legal dispute**: Earthgrains moved for summary judgment  
  * Argued plaintiffs bound to arbitrate under collective bargaining agreement (CBA)  
  * District court agreed and granted summary judgment

### **B. The Rule 11 Issue** {#b.-the-rule-11-issue}

* **District court action**: Issued show cause order *sua sponte* (on its own)  
  * Asked why Hunter shouldn't be sanctioned  
  * **Ultimately SUSPENDED Hunter from practice for 5 years**  
  * Found legal arguments "frivolous"  
* **Why court thought frivolous**: Two years earlier, Fourth Circuit held in *Austin v. Owens-Brockway* (1996):  
  * General CBA arbitration clauses DO apply to statutory discrimination claims  
  * Hunter's case filed in Fourth Circuit  
  * *Austin* was controlling precedent directly against her

### **C. The Key Rule 11(b)(2) Question** {#c.-the-key-rule-11(b)(2)-question}

**Hunter's argument**: CBAs with general arbitration language shouldn't cover Title VII claims

**Was this:**

* Warranted by existing law? **NO** \- *Austin* directly contrary  
* Nonfrivolous argument for changing law? **THIS WAS THE FIGHT**

### **D. Hunter's Basis for Arguing Change** {#d.-hunter's-basis-for-arguing-change}

1. **Supreme Court precedent**: *Alexander v. Gardner-Denver* (1974) supported her position  
2. **Circuit split**: Six other circuits (2nd, 6th, 7th, 8th, 10th, 11th) rejected the *Austin* approach

**Weakness**: She did NOT cite the six contrary circuits

* Weakened presentation  
* But didn't make argument frivolous

### **E. Fourth Circuit Holding: SANCTIONS VACATED** {#e.-fourth-circuit-holding:-sanctions-vacated}

**Argument was NON-FRIVOLOUS because:**

1. **Circuit split existed**  
   * When reasonable judges in multiple circuits disagree, how can argument be unreasonable?  
2. **Supreme Court authority supported her**  
   * Relied on *Alexander* (legitimate Supreme Court decision)  
3. **Attorneys can argue for change**  
   * Under Rule 11(b)(2), attorneys are "plainly entitled (and probably obligated)" to argue controlling precedent is wrong  
   * Especially when they can seek:  
     * En banc review  
     * Supreme Court review  
4. **Supreme Court ultimately agreed with her**  
   * After she filed but before sanctions imposed  
   * *Wright v. Universal Maritime Service Corp.* (1998) validated her legal theory

### **F. En Banc Review Explained** {#f.-en-banc-review-explained}

**En banc** \= "Full bench" \- case heard by ALL judges of court of appeals

**Normal process:**

* Three-judge panel randomly assigned  
* Panel decision becomes binding circuit precedent

**When en banc granted:**

1. Panel decision conflicts with prior circuit precedent  
2. Case involves question of "exceptional importance"  
3. Circuit wants to overrule its own precedent (only full court can do this)

**In Hunter**: She was entitled to argue *Austin* was wrong because she could:

1. Expect district court to follow *Austin* (it's binding)  
2. Hope to convince a panel to take the case en banc  
3. If en banc failed, seek Supreme Court review based on circuit split

**This is the legitimate pathway for changing circuit precedent from within**

**Practical notes:**

* En banc review is **rarely granted** \- most petitions denied  
* Circuit size varies:  
  * Ninth Circuit: 29 active judges (uses "limited en banc" of 11 judges)  
  * Sixth Circuit (includes Ohio): 16 active judges

### **G. Critical Takeaways for Rule 11** {#g.-critical-takeaways-for-rule-11}

| Principle | Application |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Losing ≠ Rule 11 violation** | Summary judgment against you doesn't automatically mean arguments were frivolous |
| **Can argue against controlling precedent** | If there's a basis (circuit split, Supreme Court dicta, law reviews, dissents), you can argue circuit got it wrong |
| **Non-frivolous doesn't mean winning** | Argument doesn't have to ultimately succeed—just needs legitimate basis |
| **Sua sponte sanctions require extra care** | No 21-day safe harbor, so courts must be especially cautious |
| **Thoroughness helps but isn't required** | Hunter's failure to cite six favorable circuits made argument weaker, but "lack of thoroughness does not render her position frivolous" |

### **H. The *Blue v. U.S. Dept. of Army* Quote** {#h.-the-blue-v.-u.s.-dept.-of-army-quote}

Chief Judge Wilkinson's observation:

"If arguing against precedent were forbidden, the parties and counsel who in the early 1950s brought the case of Brown v. Board of Education might have been thought by some district court to have engaged in sanctionable conduct for pursuing their claims in the face of the contrary precedent of Plessy v. Ferguson. The civil rights movement might have died aborning."

### **I. Connection to Course Themes** {#i.-connection-to-course-themes}

**The tension in Rule 11(b)(2):**

* Attorneys must have legal basis  
* BUT rule can't be so strict it chills legitimate advocacy for legal change  
* **Standard**: Whether "a reasonable attorney in like circumstances could not have believed his actions to be legally justified"  
* **Objective reasonableness** standard, not whether you ultimately win

### **J. Sources for "Nonfrivolous Argument for Change"** {#j.-sources-for-"nonfrivolous-argument-for-change"}

**You can rely on:**

* Circuit splits  
* Supreme Court dicta  
* Law review articles  
* Dissenting opinions  
* Scholarly commentary  
* Trends in other jurisdictions

## **XI. RULE 11(c) \- SANCTIONS** {#xi.-rule-11(c)---sanctions}

### **A. Who Can Be Sanctioned \- Rule 11(c)(1)** {#a.-who-can-be-sanctioned---rule-11(c)(1)}

**Subject to sanctions:**

* Attorneys  
* Law firms  
* Parties  
* **Exception**: "Absent exceptional circumstances"

**Important limitation from Rule 11(c)(5)(A):**

* **CANNOT** impose monetary sanctions on **represented party** for Rule 11(b)(2) violations (legal basis problems)  
* Only the **lawyer** can be sanctioned for bad legal arguments

### **B. Types of Sanctions Available \- Rule 11(c)(4) and (5)** {#b.-types-of-sanctions-available---rule-11(c)(4)-and-(5)}

| What Court Can Impose | Limits |
| ----- | ----- |
| Nonmonetary directives (reprimand, CLE) | Must be "sufficient to deter" \- no more |
| Penalty paid to court | Same |
| Attorney's fees to movant | Only if motion-based (NOT sua sponte) |

**Key principle**: Sanctions focused on **deterrence**, not compensation

### **C. Procedures for Rule 11 Sanctions** {#c.-procedures-for-rule-11-sanctions}

### **Pathway 1: Motion by Opposing Party \- Rule 11(c)(2)** {#pathway-1:-motion-by-opposing-party---rule-11(c)(2)}

**Step 1**: Opposing party drafts motion describing specific conduct violating Rule 11(b)

**Step 2**: **SERVE** motion on offending party \- but **DO NOT FILE** with court yet

**Step 3**: **21-Day Safe Harbor**

* Offending party has 21 days to withdraw or correct challenged paper

**Step 4**: If paper withdrawn/corrected → motion dies; cannot file it

**Step 5**: If NOT withdrawn/corrected after 21 days → file motion with court FILE IT ON ITS OWN.

**Step 6**: Court decides after notice and opportunity to respond

**Critical requirements:**

* Motion must be filed as **separate motion** (not bundled with other motions)  
* Must actually serve on opposing party first  
* Must wait full 21 days before filing

### **Pathway 2: Court Acts Sua Sponte \- Rule 11(c)(3)** {#pathway-2:-court-acts-sua-sponte---rule-11(c)(3)}

**Step 1**: Court issues **show cause order** describing specific conduct

**Step 2**: Attorney/party must respond explaining why conduct didn't violate Rule 11(b)

**Step 3**: Court decides

**Key differences:**

* **NO safe harbor** (can't withdraw to escape)  
* Advisory Committee: Should be reserved for conduct "akin to contempt of court"  
* **CANNOT** award attorney's fees to movant (only penalties to court or nonmonetary sanctions)

### **D. Rule 11(d) \- Inapplicable to Discovery** {#d.-rule-11(d)---inapplicable-to-discovery}

**Important**: Rule 11 does NOT apply to discovery

* Discovery has its own sanctions regime (Rule 37\)

### **E. What Is NOT Subject to Rule 11** {#e.-what-is-not-subject-to-rule-11}

**Oral arguments are NOT subject to Rule 11**

* Only applies to signed, filed, or submitted documents  
* Can make arguments orally without Rule 11 exposure

## **XII. RULE 11 PRACTICE QUESTIONS** {#xii.-rule-11-practice-questions}

### **Question 1: Oral Argument Mistakes** {#question-1:-oral-argument-mistakes}

**Facts**: Attorney filed motion to dismiss under 12(b)(6). In oral argument, judge said maybe there's a problem with subject matter jurisdiction. Attorney agreed but made mistakes on the law of subject matter jurisdiction. Should attorney be sanctioned?

A. Yes, because attorney should have said was not sure of the law

B. No, because mistakes were in oral argument, rather than written document

**Answer: B \- No**

* Rule 11 applies to documents (signed, filed, submitted, advocated)  
* Does NOT apply to oral arguments

### **Question 2: Who Can Be Sanctioned** {#question-2:-who-can-be-sanctioned}

**Facts**: Atty 1 drafts and files complaint with legal mistake \- sues plaintiff's employer for negligence, when only workers' compensation is available. Atty 2 argues (but did not draft or file) to court that claim is legally valid. Who is subject to Rule 11 sanctions?

A. Atty 1 only

B. Atty 2 only

C. Both Atty 1 and Atty 2 and their law firm, absent exceptional circumstances

D. Both Atty 1 and Atty 2 and their law firm, absent exceptional circumstances, and Client

E. Both Atty 1 and Atty 2, but not their law firm or their Client

**Answer: C**

* Rule 11(c)(1): Can sanction anyone who "presents" a document  
* "Presenting" includes signing, filing, submitting, OR **later advocating**  
* Both attorneys presented the document  
* Law firm can be sanctioned absent exceptional circumstances  
* Client CANNOT be sanctioned for Rule 11(b)(2) violation (legal basis problem) \- Rule 11(c)(5)(A)

## **XVI. KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR EXAM AND PRACTICE** {#xvi.-key-takeaways-for-exam-and-practice}

### **A. Pleading Strategy** {#a.-pleading-strategy}

**As Plaintiff:**

1. Err on side of fact pleading  
2. Plead specific facts for EVERY element  
3. Avoid conclusory allegations  
4. Tell a coherent story  
5. Ensure evidentiary support exists (Rule 11(b)(3))  
6. Research law thoroughly (Rule 11(b)(2))

**As Defendant:**

1. File 12(b)(6) motions more aggressively post-*Twombly-Iqbal*  
2. Target conclusory allegations  
3. Highlight alternative innocent inferences  
4. Consider Rule 11 motion if appropriate (with safe harbor)

### **B. Exam Analysis Framework** {#b.-exam-analysis-framework}

**For any pleading issue:**

1. **Identify the standard**  
   * Notice pleading (Ohio) or plausibility pleading (Federal)  
2. **Apply *Twombly-Iqbal* two-step** (if federal)  
   * Step 1: Strip conclusory allegations  
   * Step 2: Assess plausibility of remaining facts  
3. **Acknowledge *Swanson* softening**  
   * "Could it have happened" vs. "more likely than not"  
   * Context matters  
4. **Check Rule 11 compliance**  
   * Rule 11(b)(2): Legal basis?  
   * Rule 11(b)(3): Evidentiary support?  
   * Rule 11(b)(1): Improper purpose?  
5. **Argue both sides**  
   * What plaintiff would argue  
   * What defendant would argue  
   * Conclude

### **C. Remember** {#c.-remember}

* **Plausibility is judge-dependent** \- shaped by judicial background and assumptions  
* **Second chance exists** \- can amend under Rule 15 if dismissed  
* **Cost-benefit investigation** \- reasonable under circumstances, not perfection  
* **Can argue for legal change** \- if legitimate basis exists  
* **Safe harbor protects** \- use 21-day period to correct mistakes

Complaint → Answer (or Motion to Dismiss) → Discovery → Summary Judgment → Trial → Post-Trial → Appeal → Supreme Court

**Key points:**

* 12(b)(6) motion comes BEFORE discovery  
* If motion granted with prejudice → case over (unless amended)  
* If motion denied → defendant files answer and discovery begins  
* Rule 11 can arise at any point when documents filed

## **KEY TAKEAWAYS** {#key-takeaways}

### **Rule 12(e) \- More Definite Statement** {#rule-12(e)---more-definite-statement}

✅ Use when complaint is

**hopelessly vague**

✅ Must show you

**cannot reasonably prepare a response**

✅ Must specify what details you need

❌ Rarely granted in modern practice (discovery fills gaps)

❌ Can't use just because complaint could be better organized

### **Rule 12(f) \- Motion to Strike** {#rule-12(f)---motion-to-strike}

✅ Use to remove

**inflammatory, improper, or irrelevant material**

✅ Use to strike

**insufficient defenses**

✅ Common targets: prejudicial language, vague damage requests

❌ Not a substitute for 12(b)(6) \- doesn't challenge legal sufficiency

❌ Courts reluctant to strike unless material is clearly improper

### **Both Are Subject to the Waiver Trap\!** {#both-are-subject-to-the-waiver-trap!}

⚠️ Both 12(e) and 12(f) are **waivable** under Rule 12(h)(1)

⚠️ Must be included in your **first Rule 12 motion** (omnibus motion rule)

⚠️ If you file a 12(b)(6) motion first and leave out 12(e) or 12(f), you've waived them\!

## **IV. THE "WAIVER TRAP" (CRITICAL CONCEPT\!)** {#iv.-the-"waiver-trap"-(critical-concept!)}

### **A. The Omnibus Motion Rule \- Rule 12(g)(2)** {#a.-the-omnibus-motion-rule---rule-12(g)(2)}

**Key Principle:** Must consolidate ALL available Rule 12 defenses in ONE motion (your first motion)

**The Rule States:**

"A party that makes a motion under this rule must not make another motion under this rule raising a defense or objection that was available to the party but omitted from its earlier motion."

### **B. What Gets Waived \- Rule 12(h)(1)** {#b.-what-gets-waived---rule-12(h)(1)}

**WAIVABLE DEFENSES** (if not raised in first Rule 12 motion OR answer):

* 12(b)(2) \- Personal jurisdiction  
* 12(b)(3) \- Venue  
* 12(b)(4) \- Insufficient process  
* 12(b)(5) \- Insufficient service of process  
* Rule 12(e) \- More definite statement  
* Rule 12(f) \- Motion to strike

### **C. What Survives \- Rule 12(h)(2) & (3)** {#c.-what-survives---rule-12(h)(2)-&-(3)}

**Rule 12(h)(2) \- Can raise later (even after answer):**

* 12(b)(6) \- Failure to state a claim  
* 12(b)(7) \- Failure to join party under Rule 19  
* Can be raised: in pleading, motion for judgment on pleadings, or at trial

**Rule 12(h)(3) \- NEVER waived:**

* 12(b)(1) \- Subject matter jurisdiction  
* Can be raised at ANY time, even on appeal

### **D. Case Example: *Hunter v. Serv-Tech, Inc.*** {#d.-case-example:-hunter-v.-serv-tech,-inc.}

**Facts:**

1. Defendant Offshore files 12(b)(5) motion (insufficient service)  
2. Plaintiff amends complaint  
3. Offshore files Answer (includes what court calls "affirmative defense" but is really a 12(b) defense)  
4. Offshore tries to file second motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction (12(b)(2))

**Issue:** Did Offshore waive 12(b)(2) by not including it in first motion?

**Offshore's Argument:** Original motion stated they "expressly reserve all rights to challenge subject matter jurisdiction and/or personal jurisdiction"

**Holding:** \[Need to check your case materials for outcome\]

## **V. THE ANSWER (Rule 8(b) & (c))** {#v.-the-answer-(rule-8(b)-&-(c))}

### **A. Four Components of an Answer** {#a.-four-components-of-an-answer}

**1\. "Leftover" Rule 12(b) Defenses**

* Any 12(b) defenses not yet waived  
* Include under Rule 8(b)(1)(A)

**2\. Admissions and Denials (Rule 8(b))**

**Three Options \- Rule 8(b)(5):**

1. **Admit** \- allegation treated as true  
2. **Deny** \- issue remains for trial  
3. **Lack of knowledge** \- insufficient information to admit or deny (functions as denial)

**Rule 8(b)(4):** If denying only part, must:

* State specifically what is true  
* Deny only the rest

**Example from *Reis Robotics*:**

* Improper response: "To the extent the alleged 'contract'... failed to warrant... it was procured by fraud... accordingly, the remaining allegations... are denied as true."  
* Problem: Didn't properly admit, deny, or state lack of knowledge

**3\. Affirmative Defenses (Rule 8(c))**

**Definition:** Concedes plaintiff's claim *arguendo* but asserts additional facts that defeat liability

**Rule 8(c) Examples:**

* Statute of limitations  
* Release  
* Comparative negligence  
* Waiver  
* Estoppel  
* Laches  
* Fraud  
* Failure of consideration  
* Unclean hands

**4\. Counterclaims (Rule 13\)**

* 13(a) \- Compulsory counterclaims  
* 13(b) \- Permissive counterclaims

## **VI. AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSES IN DEPTH** {#vi.-affirmative-defenses-in-depth}

### **A. Key Principle: *Ingraham v. United States*** {#a.-key-principle:-ingraham-v.-united-states}

**The "Lie Behind a Log" Principle:**

* Cannot ambush plaintiff with surprise defenses  
* Affirmative defenses MUST be pleaded in answer or they're WAIVED  
* Can't raise them for first time on appeal

**Facts of *Ingraham*:**

* Plaintiffs won multi-million dollar judgments  
* On appeal, U.S. argued judgment capped at $500,000  
* Plaintiff's argument: U.S. never raised this cap as affirmative defense in answer  
* **Result:** U.S. waived the defense

### **B. Case Example: *Reis Robotics USA v. Concept Industries*** {#b.-case-example:-reis-robotics-usa-v.-concept-industries}

**Motion to Strike Insufficient "Affirmative Defenses":**

**2nd Affirmative Defense:** "Reis breached the contract... and that contract may be void for fraud and/or failure of consideration"

* Issue: Is this really an affirmative defense or a denial?

**3rd Affirmative Defense:** "Reis's payment claims... are barred because Concept never authorized Reis to begin manufacturing"

* Issue: Sounds like a denial, not affirmative defense

**5th Affirmative Defense:** "Claims are barred or limited by laches, waiver, estoppel, and unclean hands, or similar legal or equitable doctrines"

* Issue: Too vague/general

**6th Affirmative Defense:** Defendant "reserves the right to add additional affirmative defenses"

* Issue: Can't "reserve" \- must plead now or waive

### **C. Practice Hypo: Billy Builder** {#c.-practice-hypo:-billy-builder}

**Facts:** Builder puts garage on property line, equipment crosses line, damages Neighbor's property. Neighbor sues.

**Classify each defense:**

1. **"Builder never went upon the property"**  
   * **DENIAL** \- contradicts plaintiff's factual allegation  
2. **"Builder had permission from Neighbor to enter"**  
   * **AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSE** \- admits entry but asserts additional fact (permission) that defeats liability  
3. **"Builder acquired property by adverse possession"**  
   * **AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSE** \- admits entry but asserts he owned the property  
4. **"Neighbor negligently failed to prune oak, causing $1,500 damage to Builder's garden"**  
   * **COUNTERCLAIM** (Rule 13\) \- Builder's own claim against Neighbor

## **IX. PRACTICE QUESTIONS (from PowerPoint)** {#ix.-practice-questions-(from-powerpoint)}

### **Question 1** {#question-1}

Def files 12(b)(6) motion and loses. Now wants to file 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(2). May she?

**Answer:**

* **12(b)(1) \- YES** \- Subject matter jurisdiction never waived (Rule 12(h)(3))  
* **12(b)(2) \- NO** \- Personal jurisdiction was "available" when filed first motion, so waived under Rule 12(g)(2) (not saved by 12(h)(2) or (h)(3))

### **Question 2** {#question-2}

Def files 12(b)(1), denied. Then moves for 12(b)(3) and 12(b)(7). Plaintiff claims waiver. Correct?

**Answer:**

* **12(b)(3) \- YES, WAIVED** \- Venue is waivable under 12(h)(1), was available in first motion  
* **12(b)(7) \- NO, NOT WAIVED** \- Failure to join party saved by Rule 12(h)(2)

### **Question 3** {#question-3}

Def filed Answer including 12(b)(1), 12(b)(3), and 12(b)(7). Can Def include these in Answer?

**Answer:**

* **YES** \- Rule 12(b) defenses that haven't been waived can be included in Answer under Rule 8(b)(1)(A)  
* All three of these can still be raised (though 12(b)(3) should have been in earlier motion if one was filed)

### **Question 4** {#question-4}

Same as Q3, but now Def wants to move for 12(b)(2) and 12(b)(6). Can she, or waived?

**Answer:**

* **12(b)(2) \- WAIVED** \- Personal jurisdiction is waivable (12(h)(1)); was available when Answer filed but omitted (Rule 12(g)(2))  
* **12(b)(6) \- NOT WAIVED** \- Failure to state claim saved by Rule 12(h)(2); can be raised even after answer

## **X. KEY TAKEAWAYS** {#x.-key-takeaways}

### **The Waiver Trap Summary Chart:** {#the-waiver-trap-summary-chart:}

| Defense | When Waived | Authority |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| 12(b)(2)-(5) | If not in first Rule 12 motion OR answer | 12(h)(1) |
| 12(e), 12(f) | If not in first Rule 12 motion OR answer | 12(h)(1) |
| 12(b)(6), (7) | Can raise through trial | 12(h)(2) |
| 12(b)(1) | NEVER waived | 12(h)(3) |

### **Strategic Considerations:** {#strategic-considerations:}

**If filing a Rule 12 motion:**

* Include ALL available 12(b), 12(e), and 12(f) defenses  
* Or risk waiving them forever

**If filing an Answer:**

* Include all admissions/denials  
* Include "leftover" 12(b) defenses not yet waived  
* Include ALL affirmative defenses (Rule 8(c)) or waive them  
* Include counterclaims

# **Week 4 Civil Procedure Outline \- Pleadings & Amendments** {#week-4-civil-procedure-outline---pleadings-&-amendments}

## **I. PLAINTIFFS' PLEADINGS** {#i.-plaintiffs'-pleadings}

### **A. The Complaint \- Purposes** {#a.-the-complaint---purposes}

* Provide notice to defendant  
* State claims for relief  
* Invoke court's jurisdiction

### **B. Form & Mechanics (Rule 11\)** {#b.-form-&-mechanics-(rule-11)}

* Certification requirements  
* Sanctions for violations  
* Care and candor obligations

### **C. Content Requirements** {#c.-content-requirements}

### **1\. Rule 8(a) \- Three Required Elements** {#1.-rule-8(a)---three-required-elements}

* **(a)(1)** Grounds for jurisdiction  
* **(a)(2)** Short, plain statement showing entitlement to relief  
* **(a)(3)** Demand for judgment/relief sought

### **2\. Rule 8(a)(2) \- Factual Allegations** {#2.-rule-8(a)(2)---factual-allegations}

* **Twombly-Iqbal Standard**: Must be plausible, not just possible  
* More than "labels and conclusions"  
* Must contain enough facts to state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face

### **3\. Rule 9(b) \- Special Pleading for Fraud** {#3.-rule-9(b)---special-pleading-for-fraud}

* Must state with particularity: who, what, when, where, how  
* Malice, intent, knowledge may be alleged generally

### **4\. Rule 12(b)(6) \- Motion to Dismiss** {#4.-rule-12(b)(6)---motion-to-dismiss}

* Tests legal sufficiency of complaint  
* Court assumes well-pleaded facts are true  
* If court considers evidence outside complaint → converts to summary judgment motion (Rule 12(d))

---

## **II. DEFENDANTS' PLEADINGS \- Three Response Options** {#ii.-defendants'-pleadings---three-response-options}

### **A. Answer the Complaint (Rules 8(b), (c), (d), (e) & 10\)** {#a.-answer-the-complaint-(rules-8(b),-(c),-(d),-(e)-&-10)}

### **1\. Admissions & Denials (Rule 8(b))** {#1.-admissions-&-denials-(rule-8(b))}

* Must admit or deny each allegation  
* Lack of knowledge \= denial (if stating that)  
* Failure to deny \= admission

### **2\. Defenses to Include** {#2.-defenses-to-include}

**a. Procedural Defenses**

* "Leftover" Rule 12(b) defenses not yet waived  
* **The Waiver Trap** (Rules 12(g) & (h)):  
  * **12(b)(2)-(5) ARE WAIVABLE**: personal jurisdiction, venue, insufficient process, insufficient service  
  * **12(b)(1), (6), (7) NOT WAIVABLE**: subject matter jurisdiction, failure to state claim, failure to join party

**b. Substantive Defenses**

* **Rule 8(c) Affirmative Defenses** \- Must be pleaded or waived  
  * Examples: statute of limitations, statute of frauds, contributory negligence, assumption of risk, res judicata, etc.  
  * Admit facts but provide reason plaintiff still loses

### **3\. Counterclaims (Rule 13\)** {#3.-counterclaims-(rule-13)}

**a. Compulsory Counterclaims \- Rule 13(a)**

* **MUST** assert or it's waived  
* Requirements:  
  * **(A)** Arises from same transaction/occurrence as plaintiff's claim; AND  
  * **(B)** Doesn't require adding party over whom court lacks jurisdiction  
* **Exception**: Claim already pending in another action

**b. Permissive Counterclaims \- Rule 13(b)**

* **MAY** assert (optional)  
* Any claim not arising from same transaction/occurrence  
* Can bring later in separate lawsuit

**c. Relief Sought \- Rule 13(c)**

* Can exceed or differ from plaintiff's requested relief

**Key Case: *King v. Blanton***

* Equitable waiver of compulsory counterclaims  
* Even if first case settled (not adjudicated), may still bar later lawsuit  
* Fairness considerations:  
  * Judicial efficiency  
  * Prejudice to opposing party  
  * Party's knowledge and conduct  
  * Opportunity to protect via settlement negotiations

---

### **B. Move to Dismiss or Strike** {#b.-move-to-dismiss-or-strike}

### **1\. Rule 12(b) Defenses (Pre-Answer Motions)** {#1.-rule-12(b)-defenses-(pre-answer-motions)}

* **(1)** Lack of subject matter jurisdiction \[NOT WAIVABLE\]  
* **(2)** Lack of personal jurisdiction \[WAIVABLE\]  
* **(3)** Improper venue \[WAIVABLE\]  
* **(4)** Insufficient process \[WAIVABLE\]  
* **(5)** Insufficient service of process \[WAIVABLE\]  
* **(6)** Failure to state a claim \[NOT WAIVABLE\]  
* **(7)** Failure to join required party \[NOT WAIVABLE\]

### **2\. Other Rule 12 Motions** {#2.-other-rule-12-motions}

* **Rule 12(e)**: Motion for more definite statement  
* **Rule 12(f)**: Motion to strike

### **3\. The "Omnibus Motion Rule" \- Rule 12(g)(2)** {#3.-the-"omnibus-motion-rule"---rule-12(g)(2)}

* Must consolidate all available 12(b) defenses in first motion  
* Waivable defenses (12(b)(2)-(5)) lost if not included

So the logic chain is:

1. **12(g)(2):** You must consolidate, with exceptions for (h)(2) and (h)(3) defenses.  
2. **12(h)(1)(A):** If you didn't consolidate a (b)(2)–(5) defense, it's waived.  
3. **12(h)(2):** (b)(6) and (b)(7) are explicitly preserved until trial.  
4. **12(h)(3):** (b)(1) is preserved indefinitely.

### **4\. Timing \- Rule 12(a)(1)** {#4.-timing---rule-12(a)(1)}

* Must file Answer or Rule 12 motion within **21 days**  
* Exception: 60 days if timely waiver of service

---

### **C. Do Nothing \- Default (Rule 55\)** {#c.-do-nothing---default-(rule-55)}

* Court may enter default judgment  
* Generally a bad idea

## **III. AMENDMENTS TO PLEADINGS (Rule 15\)** {#iii.-amendments-to-pleadings-(rule-15)}

### **A. Amendments Before Trial \- Rule 15(a)** {#a.-amendments-before-trial---rule-15(a)}

### **1\. As a Matter of Course \- Rule 15(a)(1) \[ONE TIME ONLY\]** {#1.-as-a-matter-of-course---rule-15(a)(1)-[one-time-only]}

Can amend once without permission within:

**Three Time Periods (whichever applies first):**

**a. First Period \- 15(a)(1)(A)**

* Within **21 days after serving** the pleading

**b. Second Period \- 15(a)(1)(B)**

* If pleading requires responsive pleading: within **21 days after** receiving:  
  * The responsive pleading; OR  
  * A Rule 12(b), (e), or (f) motion (whichever is earlier)

**Pleadings requiring responses:**

* Complaint (requires Answer)  
* Answer with counterclaim (requires Answer/Reply)  
* Answer with crossclaim (requires Answer)  
* Third-party complaint (requires Answer)

**c. Third Period**

* If defendant files 12(b) motion instead of Answer: 21 days after motion filed

**IMPORTANT**: Only ONE amendment as matter of course \- periods not cumulative

### **2\. Other Amendments \- Rule 15(a)(2)** {#2.-other-amendments---rule-15(a)(2)}

After 21-day periods expire, need:

* **Written consent** of opposing party; OR  
* **Court's leave** (permission)

**Standard**: "Court should freely give leave when justice so requires"

**Factors Courts Consider** (*Foman v. Davis*):

* Undue delay  
* Bad faith or dilatory motive  
* Repeated failure to cure deficiencies  
* **Undue prejudice** to opposing party (preparation prejudice, not merits)  
* **Futility** of amendment (e.g., statute of limitations has run; fails to state claim)

**Key Case: *Beeck v. Aqua Slide 'N' Dive***

* Liberal standard for granting leave  
* Focus on prejudice and futility

---

### **B. Amendments During & After Trial \- Rule 15(b)** {#b.-amendments-during-&-after-trial---rule-15(b)}

### **1\. Based on Objection at Trial \- 15(b)(1)** {#1.-based-on-objection-at-trial---15(b)(1)}

* If party objects evidence not in pleadings  
* Court **should freely permit** amendment if:  
  * Aids in presenting merits; AND  
  * Objecting party fails to show prejudice  
* Court may grant continuance to meet new evidence

### **2\. Issues Tried by Consent \- 15(b)(2)** {#2.-issues-tried-by-consent---15(b)(2)}

* Issue not in pleadings but tried with **express or implied consent**  
* **Treated as if raised in pleadings**  
* Can move to amend even after judgment  
* Failure to amend doesn't affect trial result on that issue

---

### **C. Amendments After Statute of Limitations Runs \- Rule 15(c)** {#c.-amendments-after-statute-of-limitations-runs---rule-15(c)}

### **1\. Relation Back \- Rule 15(c)(1)** {#1.-relation-back---rule-15(c)(1)}

Amendment relates back to original filing date when:

**a. Rule 15(c)(1)(A)**

* Applicable law allows relation back

**b. Rule 15(c)(1)(B) \- New Claim Against Original Parties**

* Amendment asserts claim/defense arising from **same conduct, transaction, or occurrence** as original pleading  
* Same factual circumstances, different legal theory

**Key Case: *Bonerb v. Richard J. Caron Foundation***

* New claims related to original facts can relate back  
* Defendant already on notice to preserve evidence  
* Still must meet 15(a)(2) standard ("justice requires")

**c. Rule 15(c)(1)(C) \- New/Corrected Defendant**

**ALL Requirements Must Be Met:**

**(i) Rule 15(c)(1)(B) satisfied** (same transaction/occurrence)

**(ii) Within 90 days of filing complaint** (Rule 4(m) period), new defendant:

* Received notice of action (won't be prejudiced in defending); AND  
* Knew or should have known action would have been brought against it but for mistake in identity

---

### **2\. Rule 15(c)(1)(C) Deep Dive** {#2.-rule-15(c)(1)(c)-deep-dive}

**What Must Happen Within 90 Days:**

**Notice Requirements:**

* New defendant must receive **actual knowledge** lawsuit was filed  
* NOT formal service required  
* Can be informal notice (related entity forwarded complaint, shared counsel, internal communications)

**"Mistake Concerning Identity":**

* About **WHO** you meant to sue, not **WHAT** they did  
* ✅ Examples: misnomer, parent/subsidiary confusion, wrong corporate entity  
* ❌ NOT: discovering new wrongdoer (that's adding new claim, not fixing mistake)

**Key Case: *Krupski v. Costa Crociere***

**Facts:**

* Feb 21, 2007: Plaintiff injured on cruise  
* Feb 1, 2008: Sued Costa Cruise Lines (CCL) \- wrong defendant  
* Feb 21, 2008: Statute of limitations expires  
* Feb 25, 2008: CCL answers, says wrong defendant  
* July 11, 2008: Plaintiff moves to amend, add Costa Crociere (correct defendant)

**11th Circuit Holding (REVERSED):**

* Plaintiff made "deliberate choice" to sue wrong party  
* Plaintiff unduly delayed

**Supreme Court Holding:**

* Rule focuses on **defendant's knowledge**, not plaintiff's  
* Question: Did new defendant know or should have known of mistake?  
* Both entities shared same attorney  
* New defendant had notice within 90 days  
* **Amendment allowed**

**Service After Amendment:**

* Must still properly serve new defendant with amended complaint  
* Relation back solves **timing** problem (statute of limitations)  
* Proper service solves **jurisdiction** problem  
* Need both

**Legal Requirements:**

* Allege facts supporting all elements of claims  
* Meet *Iqbal* plausibility standard  
* Include enough facts to make claim plausible, not just possible

# **Week 5 Federal Litigation Outline** {#week-5-federal-litigation-outline}

## **I. AMENDMENTS TO PLEADINGS — Rule 15** {#i.-amendments-to-pleadings-—-rule-15}

### **A. Amendments Before Trial** {#a.-amendments-before-trial}

**1\. As a Matter of Course — Rule 15(a)(1)**

* Automatic right, no permission needed  
* Within 21 days after serving the pleading, OR  
* Within 21 days after service of responsive pleading or Rule 12(b), (e), (f) motion (whichever is earlier)  
* Can amend Answer to add waivable 12(b)(2)-(5) defenses under 12(h)(1)(B)

**2\. Other Amendments — Rule 15(a)(2)**

* After 21-day windows expire, need either:  
  * Opposing party's written consent, OR  
  * Court's leave  
* Standard: "freely given when justice so requires"  
* **Foman v. Davis, 371 U.S. 178 (1962)** — leave granted absent:  
  * Undue delay  
  * Bad faith  
  * Repeated failure to cure  
  * Undue prejudice  
  * Futility of amendment

**3\. Amendments During/After Trial — Rule 15(b)**

### **B. Relation Back — Rule 15(c)** {#b.-relation-back-—-rule-15(c)}

**Purpose:** Allows amendments after the statute of limitations has run by "relating back" to the original filing date

**1\. Rule 15(c)(1)(A):** Applicable law allows relation back

**2\. Rule 15(c)(1)(B):** Amendment arises out of same conduct, transaction, or occurrence as original pleading

**3\. Rule 15(c)(1)(C):** Changing/adding a party — requires ALL of:

* 15(c)(1)(B) satisfied (same transaction/occurrence)  
* Within Rule 4(m) 90-day service window, new party:  
  * (i) Received sufficient notice — won't be prejudiced in defending on merits  
  * (ii) Knew or should have known action would have been brought against it but for a mistake  
* **Krupski v. Costa Crociere, 560 U.S. 538 (2010)** — "mistake" inquiry focuses on what the **prospective new defendant** knew/should have known, NOT plaintiff's diligence

### **C. Exam Framework for Amendment Questions** {#c.-exam-framework-for-amendment-questions}

15(a)(1) — Matter of course? (usually no — quick disposal)

│  NO

└─ 15(a)(2) — Leave of court (Foman factors)

    ├─ Quick pass through 4 factors

    └─ Futility? → Opens Rule 15(c) relation back analysis

        ├─ SOL expired?

        ├─ 15(c)(1)(B) — Same transaction/occurrence?

        ├─ 15(c)(1)(C)(i) — Notice within 4(m) window?

        └─ 15(c)(1)(C)(ii) — Mistake? (Krupski — MOST INK)

            │

            ALL MET → Not futile → Back to Foman → GRANTED

## **II. DISCOVERY IN FEDERAL COURT — Rules 26-37** {#ii.-discovery-in-federal-court-—-rules-26-37}

### **A. Informal Discovery** {#a.-informal-discovery}

* Pre-formal discovery investigation (e.g., Gaylord v. Homemakers)

**Gaylord v. Homemakers Key Holdings:**

* Rule 4.2 (no-contact rule) not violated when:  
  * No lawsuit yet filed (not yet a "party")  
  * No knowledge opposing party had counsel  
* Even IF ethics violation occurred → evidence NOT excluded  
  * Ethics rules govern lawyer discipline, NOT evidence admissibility  
  * No exclusionary rule for ethics violations

### **B. Scope of Discovery — Rule 26(b)(1)** {#b.-scope-of-discovery-—-rule-26(b)(1)}

**Three requirements (ALL must be met):**

**1\. Nonprivileged**

* Attorney-client privilege  
* Work product doctrine (Rule 26(b)(3))  
* Spousal privilege  
* Doctor-patient / psychotherapist-patient privilege  
* Fifth Amendment privilege  
* Clergy-penitent privilege  
* If privileged → discovery stops, full stop

**2\. Relevant to Any Party's Claim or Defense**

* Broader than trial relevance  
* Narrower than old "subject matter" standard (2000 amendment)  
* Court can expand to subject matter relevance for good cause  
* **Discoverability ≠ Admissibility** — information need not be admissible to be discoverable

**3\. Proportional to the Needs of the Case**

* Elevated to rule text in 2015 amendments  
* Six factors:  
  1. Importance of the issues at stake  
  2. Amount in controversy  
  3. Parties' relative access to relevant information  
  4. Parties' resources  
  5. Importance of discovery in resolving the issues  
  6. Burden/expense vs. likely benefit  
* Burden is shared: requester shows relevance, responder shows disproportionality

**Key Principles:**

* Producing party pays for discovery  
* Privilege protects **communications**, NOT underlying **facts**  
* Privileges are NOT self-executing — must affirmatively assert \+ provide privilege log per Rule 26(b)(5)(A)

Is information discoverable?

Step 1: Privileged? → YES \= Stop ❌

Step 2: Relevant to claim/defense? → NO \= Stop ❌

Step 3: Proportional? → NO \= Stop ❌

All YES → DISCOVERABLE ✅ (need not be admissible)

### **C. Limits on Discovery** {#c.-limits-on-discovery}

1. **Rule 26(b)(1)** — Privilege, irrelevance, disproportionality  
2. **Rule 26(b)(2)(B)** — ESI not reasonably accessible  
3. **Rule 26(b)(2)(C)** — Cumulative, duplicative, obtainable more conveniently, or ample prior opportunity  
4. **Rule 26(b)(3)** — Work product / trial preparation  
5. **Rules 26(a)(2) & (b)(4)** — Experts not intended to testify  
6. **Rule 26(c)** — Protective orders  
7. **Discovery tools** — Presumptive limits on depositions, interrogatories, document requests, requests to admit, exams

### **D. Motions** {#d.-motions}

* **Rule 37(a)** — Motion to compel  
* **Rule 26(c)** — Motion for protective order

### **E. Sanctions** {#e.-sanctions}

* **Rule 37** — Discovery sanctions  
* **Rule 26(g)** — Certification sanctions  
* **Spoliation** — Common law \+ Rule 37(e) (ESI)

## **III. WORK PRODUCT DOCTRINE — Rule 26(b)(3)** {#iii.-work-product-doctrine-—-rule-26(b)(3)}

**Foundation:** *Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947)*

* If attorneys must hand over everything, they stop writing things down  
* Demoralizes legal profession; harms clients and justice

### **A. The Six Elements (Exam Checklist)** {#a.-the-six-elements-(exam-checklist)}

**Element \[1\]: Document or Tangible Thing**

* Memos, notes, emails, photos, reports, ESI ✅  
* Facts in someone's memory ❌

**Element \[2\]: Prepared in Anticipation of Litigation or for Trial** *(BIGGEST BATTLEGROUND)*

Three approaches to "anticipation of litigation":

| Approach | Standard | Source |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Specific Claim** (narrowest) | Specific claim supported by concrete facts likely leading to litigation | *Coastal States Gas Corp.* |
| **Primary Purpose** (middle) | Primary motivation was litigation preparation, not business/regulatory | Majority approach |
| **Ad Hoc** (broadest) | Totality of circumstances; absence of specific claim is just one factor | *In re Sealed Case* |

**Key issue:** Dual-purpose documents — argue which purpose was primary

**Element \[3\]: By or For a Party or Its Representative**

* Attorney, consultant, surety, indemnitor, insurer, agent

**Element \[4\]: Otherwise Discoverable Under 26(b)(1)**

* Must still meet nonprivileged, relevant, proportional

**Element \[5\]: Overcoming Protection — Substantial Need \+ No Substantial Equivalent**

* Requesting party must show BOTH:  
  * Substantial need for the materials  
  * Cannot obtain substantial equivalent without undue hardship  
* Met when: witness dead, memory lost, conditions changed, evidence destroyed  
* NOT met when: witness available for deposition, can hire own expert, other discovery available

**Element \[6\]: Even If Overcome → Court MUST Protect:**

* **(a) Opinion Work Product** (near-absolute protection):  
  * Mental impressions  
  * Conclusions  
  * Opinions  
  * Legal theories  
* **(b) Previous Statements** — Rule 26(b)(3)(C):  
  * Any person can obtain their OWN prior statement without any special showing

### **B. Two Tiers of Protection** {#b.-two-tiers-of-protection}

| Type | Protection Level | Can Be Overcome? |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Fact Work Product** | Qualified | Yes — with substantial need \+ no equivalent |
| **Opinion Work Product** | Near-absolute | Virtually never discoverable |

### **C. Exam Framework** {#c.-exam-framework}

WORK PRODUCT ANALYSIS

1\. Document/tangible thing? \[Usually quick\]

2\. Prepared in anticipation of litigation? \[MOST TIME HERE\]

   → Argue BOTH sides

   → Apply at least 2 of 3 approaches

   → Watch for dual-purpose documents

3\. By party or representative? \[Identify who \+ role\]

4\. ALL YES → Protected as work product

   → State conclusion, then pivot

5\. Can requester overcome?

   → Substantial need — both sides

   → No substantial equivalent — both sides

   → Key facts: witness dead? Evidence gone?

6\. Even if overcome → MUST protect:

   → Opinion work product (impressions, theories)

   → Statements (26(b)(3)(C))

## **V. KEY EXAM TIPS** {#v.-key-exam-tips}

1. **Always argue both sides** at every step  
2. **IRAC every element** — state rule, apply facts, address counterarguments, conclude  
3. **Cite Foman and Krupski by name** for amendment questions  
4. **Cite Hickman** for work product questions

## **PART I: APPLICABLE FEDERAL RULES OF CIVIL PROCEDURE** {#part-i:-applicable-federal-rules-of-civil-procedure}

### **A. FRCP 26(a)(1) — Mandatory Initial Disclosures** {#a.-frcp-26(a)(1)-—-mandatory-initial-disclosures}

1. **General Obligation** (Rule 26(a)(1)(A))  
   * Parties must provide disclosures **without awaiting a discovery request**  
   * Four categories of required disclosures:  
     * (i) Names, addresses, and telephone numbers of individuals with discoverable information the disclosing party may use to support its claims or defenses (excluding impeachment-only witnesses)  
     * (ii) Copies or descriptions (by category and location) of documents, ESI, and tangible things in the party's possession, custody, or control that it may use to support its claims or defenses  
     * (iii) Computation of each category of damages claimed, with supporting documents available for inspection under Rule 34  
     * (iv) Insurance agreements under which an insurer may be liable to satisfy a judgment  
2. **Exempt Proceedings** (Rule 26(a)(1)(B))  
   * Nine categories exempt from initial disclosure, including:  
     * Administrative record review actions  
     * Forfeiture actions in rem  
     * Habeas corpus petitions  
     * Pro se prisoner actions  
     * Actions to enforce/quash administrative summons or subpoena  
     * U.S. actions to recover benefit payments or collect student loans  
     * Ancillary proceedings  
     * Actions to enforce arbitration awards  
3. **Timing** (Rules 26(a)(1)(C)–(D))  
   * General: Within **14 days** after the Rule 26(f) conference (unless stipulated, ordered, or objected to)  
   * Later-joined parties: Within **30 days** after being served or joined  
4. **Basis and Unacceptable Excuses** (Rule 26(a)(1)(E))  
   * Must disclose based on information **then reasonably available**  
   * Not excused by: incomplete investigation, challenges to other party's disclosures, or other party's non-disclosure

### **B. FRCP 26(a)(2) — Disclosure of Expert Testimony** {#b.-frcp-26(a)(2)-—-disclosure-of-expert-testimony}

1. **General Obligation** (Rule 26(a)(2)(A))  
   * Must disclose identity of any witness who may present evidence under FRE 702, 703, or 705  
2. **Witnesses Requiring Written Reports** (Rule 26(a)(2)(B))  
   * Applies to: retained/specially employed experts or employees whose duties regularly involve expert testimony  
   * Report must contain:  
     * (i) Complete statement of all opinions and their basis/reasons  
     * (ii) Facts or data considered  
     * (iii) Exhibits to be used  
     * (iv) Qualifications and publications (previous 10 years)  
     * (v) List of cases with expert testimony (previous 4 years)  
     * (vi) Compensation statement  
3. **Witnesses Not Requiring Written Reports** (Rule 26(a)(2)(C))  
   * Must disclose:  
     * (i) Subject matter of expected testimony  
     * (ii) Summary of facts and opinions  
4. **Timing** (Rule 26(a)(2)(D))  
   * As court orders; absent order:  
     * At least **90 days** before trial  
     * Rebuttal evidence: within **30 days** after other party's disclosure  
5. **Supplementation** (Rule 26(a)(2)(E))  
   * Must supplement as required under Rule 26(e)

### **C. FRCP 26(b)(4)(A)–(C) — Discovery from Experts** {#c.-frcp-26(b)(4)(a)–(c)-—-discovery-from-experts}

1. **Deposition of Testifying Experts** (Rule 26(b)(4)(A))  
   * May depose any identified testifying expert  
   * If a report is required, deposition may occur **only after the report is provided**  
2. **Protection of Draft Reports** (Rule 26(b)(4)(B))  
   * Drafts of expert reports/disclosures are protected as work product  
3. **Protection of Attorney-Expert Communications** (Rule 26(b)(4)(C))  
   * Communications between attorney and reporting expert are protected **except**:  
     * (i) Communications about compensation  
     * (ii) Facts/data provided by attorney that the expert considered  
     * (iii) Assumptions provided by attorney that the expert relied on

### **D. FRCP 26(e) — Duty to Supplement Disclosures and Responses** {#d.-frcp-26(e)-—-duty-to-supplement-disclosures-and-responses}

1. **General Duty** (Rule 26(e)(1))  
   * Must supplement or correct disclosures and discovery responses:  
     * (A) In a timely manner if materially incomplete or incorrect (and corrective information not otherwise known to other parties)  
     * (B) As ordered by the court  
2. **Expert Witnesses** (Rule 26(e)(2))  
   * Duty extends to both the report and deposition testimony  
   * Additions/changes due by the time pretrial disclosures under Rule 26(a)(3) are due

### **E. FRCP 30 — Depositions by Oral Examination** {#e.-frcp-30-—-depositions-by-oral-examination}

1. **When Depositions May Be Taken** (Rule 30(a))  
   * **Without leave**: Any person, including a party; attendance compelled by subpoena under Rule 45  
   * **With leave required**:  
     * More than 10 depositions per side  
     * Deponent already deposed in the case  
     * Before the time specified in Rule 26(d) (unless deponent leaving the U.S.)  
     * Deponent confined in prison  
2. **Notice Requirements** (Rule 30(b))  
   * Reasonable written notice to every party: time, place, deponent's name/address  
   * Subpoena duces tecum materials must be listed in notice or attachment  
   * Must state method of recording (audio, audiovisual, or stenographic)  
   * May be taken by remote means (telephone, video) by stipulation or court order  
   * **Organizational depositions** (Rule 30(b)(6)): Name the entity; describe matters with reasonable particularity; entity designates witness(es); parties must confer in good faith  
3. **Officer's Duties** (Rule 30(b)(5))  
   * On-the-record statement: officer's name/address, date/time/place, deponent's name, oath administration, persons present  
   * Must avoid distortion of appearance/demeanor in recording  
   * Must state on record when deposition is complete  
4. **Examination, Objections, and Written Questions** (Rule 30(c))  
   * Examination proceeds as at trial under FRE (except Rules 103 and 615\)  
   * Objections noted on record; examination continues subject to objection  
   * Objections must be **concise, nonargumentative, and nonsuggestive**  
   * Instruction not to answer permitted **only** to:  
     * Preserve privilege  
     * Enforce a court-ordered limitation  
     * Present a motion under Rule 30(d)(3)  
5. **Duration and Sanctions** (Rule 30(d))  
   * Limited to **1 day of 7 hours** (unless stipulated or ordered otherwise)  
   * Court may sanction persons who impede, delay, or frustrate the examination  
   * Motion to terminate or limit on grounds of bad faith, annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression  
6. **Review and Changes** (Rule 30(e))  
   * Deponent has **30 days** to review transcript/recording and sign a statement of changes (form or substance) with reasons  
7. **Certification, Delivery, and Filing** (Rule 30(f))  
   * Officer certifies the witness was sworn and testimony accurately recorded  
   * Documents produced during deposition marked for identification and attached  
   * Attorney stores transcript under protective conditions  
8. **Failure to Attend** (Rule 30(g))  
   * Party who attends expecting a deposition may recover reasonable expenses if noticing party fails to attend or fails to subpoena a nonparty deponent

### **F. FRCP 33 — Interrogatories to Parties** {#f.-frcp-33-—-interrogatories-to-parties}

1. **Number and Scope** (Rule 33(a))  
   * Maximum **25 interrogatories** (including discrete subparts) unless stipulated or ordered otherwise  
   * May relate to any matter within Rule 26(b) scope  
   * Contention interrogatories (opinion/contention relating to fact or application of law to fact) are **not objectionable** per se, but court may defer answer  
2. **Answers and Objections** (Rule 33(b))  
   * Answered by the party or (for entities) by any officer or agent with available information  
   * Response due within **30 days**  
   * Each interrogatory answered **separately and fully in writing under oath**  
   * Objections stated **with specificity**; unstated grounds are **waived** (absent good cause)  
   * Answers signed by the answering person; objections signed by attorney  
3. **Use** (Rule 33(c))  
   * Answers usable to the extent allowed by FRE  
4. **Option to Produce Business Records** (Rule 33(d))  
   * If the answer can be derived from business records and the burden is substantially the same for either party, the responding party may specify records for the interrogating party to examine

### **G. FRCP 34 — Requests for Production of Documents/Things (Parties)** {#g.-frcp-34-—-requests-for-production-of-documents/things-(parties)}

1. **Scope** (Rule 34(a))  
   * Documents, ESI, tangible things in the responding party's **possession, custody, or control**  
   * Entry onto land or property for inspection, measurement, survey, photography, testing, or sampling  
2. **Procedure** (Rule 34(b))  
   * Request must describe items with **reasonable particularity**  
   * Must specify reasonable time, place, and manner  
   * May specify form for ESI  
   * Response due within **30 days**  
   * Must state compliance or specific grounds for objection  
   * Objection must state whether responsive materials are being withheld  
   * Production: as kept in usual course of business **or** organized and labeled to correspond to request categories  
   * ESI: produced in ordinary form or reasonably usable form; need not produce same ESI in more than one form  
3. **Nonparties** (Rule 34(c))  
   * Compelled via **Rule 45 subpoena**

### **H. FRCP 35 — Physical and Mental Examinations** {#h.-frcp-35-—-physical-and-mental-examinations}

1. **Order for Examination** (Rule 35(a))  
   * Court may order a party (or person in party's custody/legal control) to submit to examination  
   * Requires **motion, good cause, and notice** to all parties and the person to be examined  
   * Order must specify time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and examiner  
2. **Examiner's Report** (Rule 35(b))  
   * Moving party must deliver report on request  
   * Report must include detailed findings, diagnoses, conclusions, and test results  
   * **Waiver of privilege**: Requesting/obtaining the report or deposing the examiner waives privilege concerning all examinations of the same condition

### **I. FRCP 36 — Requests for Admissions** {#i.-frcp-36-—-requests-for-admissions}

1. **Scope and Procedure** (Rule 36(a))  
   * May request admission of: facts, application of law to fact, opinions, or genuineness of documents  
   * Each matter stated separately  
   * **Deemed admitted** if no written answer or objection served within **30 days**  
   * Answer must specifically deny or explain inability to admit/deny (after reasonable inquiry)  
   * May not object solely because the request presents a genuine issue for trial  
   * Court may determine sufficiency of answer/objection on motion  
2. **Effect of Admission** (Rule 36(b))  
   * **Conclusively established** for the pending action  
   * Withdrawal/amendment permitted only if it promotes the merits and does not prejudice the requesting party  
   * Not an admission for any other purpose or proceeding

### **J. FRCP 37(c)(1) — Failure to Disclose or Supplement** {#j.-frcp-37(c)(1)-—-failure-to-disclose-or-supplement}

1. **Self-Executing Sanction**  
   * Undisclosed information or witness **excluded** from use on motion, at hearing, or at trial  
   * Exception: failure was **substantially justified or harmless**  
2. **Additional Sanctions** (on motion, with opportunity to be heard)  
   * (A) Payment of reasonable expenses including attorney's fees  
   * (B) Informing the jury of the failure  
   * (C) Other appropriate sanctions (including Rule 37(b)(2)(A)(i)–(vi) orders)

### **K. FRCP 45 — Subpoenas (Nonparties)** {#k.-frcp-45-—-subpoenas-(nonparties)}

1. **Form and Contents** (Rule 45(a))  
   * Must state: issuing court, action title and number, command (attend/testify, produce documents/ESI/things, permit inspection), and text of Rules 45(d) and (e)  
   * May combine commands to produce with attendance commands or issue separately  
   * Must issue from the court where the action is pending  
2. **Service** (Rule 45(b))  
   * Served by any person at least 18 and not a party  
   * Must tender 1 day's attendance fees and mileage (unless U.S. government)  
   * May be served anywhere in the United States  
3. **Place of Compliance** (Rule 45(c))  
   * Trial/hearing/deposition: within **100 miles** of residence/employment/regular business (or within the state for parties/officers or trial attendance without substantial expense)  
   * Document production: within **100 miles** of residence/employment/regular business  
4. **Protections** (Rule 45(d))  
   * Issuing party must avoid **undue burden or expense**  
   * Appearance not required for document-only production  
   * Objections served before the earlier of compliance time or 14 days after service  
   * **Mandatory quashing/modification**: insufficient time, beyond geographic limits, privileged matter, undue burden  
   * **Permissive quashing/modification**: trade secrets, confidential commercial information, unretained expert opinions  
   * Alternative: court may order compliance under specified conditions if substantial need shown and reasonable compensation ensured  
5. **Duties in Responding** (Rule 45(e))  
   * Documents produced as kept in ordinary course or organized/labeled to match demand categories  
   * ESI in ordinary form or reasonably usable form; not required in more than one form  
   * Inaccessible ESI: responding person may identify sources as not reasonably accessible; court may still order production on good cause showing  
   * Privilege/protection claims must be expressly made with sufficient description  
6. **Contempt** (Rule 45(g))  
   * Court may hold in contempt a person who fails without adequate excuse to obey a subpoena

## **PART II: DISCOVERY FROM EXPERTS** {#part-ii:-discovery-from-experts}

### **A. Categorizing Experts for Discovery Purposes** {#a.-categorizing-experts-for-discovery-purposes}

1. **Five Categories of Experts**  
   * (a) **Testifying experts** — retained experts who will testify at trial  
   * (b) **Non-testifying (consulting) experts** — retained/employed only for trial preparation, not to testify  
   * (c) **Consulted but not retained experts** — contacted but not employed  
   * (d) **Fact witness (percipient) experts** — have personal knowledge of events giving rise to the lawsuit  
   * (e) **Independent/unretained experts** — neither perceived facts nor retained/consulted by any party

### **B. Discovery of Testifying Experts** {#b.-discovery-of-testifying-experts}

1. **Rationale for Broad Discovery**  
   * Effective cross-examination requires advance preparation  
   * Cannot anticipate opposing expert's approach or data without disclosure  
   * Expert witnesses may be more experienced in court than the cross-examining lawyer  
2. **Policy**: Level the playing field between experts and cross-examiners  
3. **Rule 26(a)(2)(B)**: Mandatory disclosure of identity, written report, qualifications, prior testimony, compensation

### **C. Restricted Discovery of Non-Testifying (Consulting) Experts** {#c.-restricted-discovery-of-non-testifying-(consulting)-experts}

1. **Rationale for Restriction**  
   * Opposing party can hire its own comparable expert  
   * Allowing discovery would give a **"free ride"** at the retaining party's expense  
2. **Rule 26(b)(4)(D)**: Discovery ordinarily prohibited

### **D. The Rule Makers' Compromise** {#d.-the-rule-makers'-compromise}

1. **Testifying experts**: Broad, mandatory disclosure (Rule 26(a)(2)(B)) — no request needed  
2. **Non-testifying experts**: Discovery ordinarily prohibited (Rule 26(b)(4)(D))  
3. **Balancing**: Difficulty of cold cross-examination vs. unfairness of free rides on opponent's trial preparation

### **E. Exception: Exceptional Circumstances** {#e.-exception:-exceptional-circumstances}

1. When the non-testifying expert is **unique** (e.g., only expert of that kind)  
2. No "other means" available to obtain equivalent facts/opinions  
3. Satisfies the "exceptional circumstances under which it is impracticable" standard of Rule 26(b)(4)(D)

---

### **F. Consulted-But-Not-Retained Experts (continued)** {#f.-consulted-but-not-retained-experts-(continued)}

1. Rule is **silent** on this category  
2. **Advisory Committee view**: Omission is intentional; these experts are protected from discovery  
3. **Alternative reading**: Rule exclusively identifies those experts whose facts/opinions **are protected**, leaving all others (including consulted-but-unretained experts) **vulnerable** to discovery  
4. **Strategic concern**: Unretained experts may have been rejected because they couldn't provide a favorable opinion — making them attractive discovery targets for the opponent

### **G. Fact Witness Who Is Also an Expert** {#g.-fact-witness-who-is-also-an-expert}

1. **Key Principle**: Only facts/opinions **acquired in anticipation of litigation** are protected — not all facts known by experts  
2. An expert who learned facts through **direct involvement or perception of events** is treated as an **ordinary fact witness**  
3. **Example**: Gene Gressman, automotive engineer who designed Toyota's Camry accelerator system — acquired knowledge as a participant, not in anticipation of litigation; may be deposed like any fact witness  
4. **Caveat on employees**: Employee status alone does not determine the analysis  
   * Corporate parties may use inside employees as testifying experts  
   * If an inside expert acquires facts/opinions **in anticipation of litigation**, expert work product protection applies regardless of employment

### **H. Independent, Unretained Experts** {#h.-independent,-unretained-experts}

1. **Scenario**: Expert (e.g., technical staff at Auto Safety Institute) not retained, consulted, or employed by any party but possesses relevant expertise  
2. **Tension**: Forcing testimony without compensation is tantamount to a **"taking" of intellectual property**  
3. **Rule 45(d)(3)(B)(ii)** provides the solution:  
   * Subpoena may be **quashed or modified** unless the discovering party:  
     * (a) Shows **substantial need** that cannot otherwise be met without undue hardship, **and**  
     * (b) Ensures the expert will be **reasonably compensated**  
4. **Bottom line**: "You can pick the independent expert's brain, but not her pocket"

### **I. Summary Table: Spectrum of Expert Discovery** {#i.-summary-table:-spectrum-of-expert-discovery}

| Expert Category | Discovery Permitted? | Key Rule/Principle |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Testifying expert** | Yes — broad, mandatory disclosure | Rule 26(a)(2)(B) |
| **Non-testifying, retained/employed** | Ordinarily no; exception for exceptional circumstances | Rule 26(b)(4)(D) |
| **Consulted but unretained** | Disputed — Rule is silent | Advisory Committee says protected; others disagree |
| **Fact witness who is also an expert** | Yes — treated as ordinary fact witness | Facts not acquired in anticipation of litigation |
| **Independent, unretained expert** | Yes, with substantial need \+ reasonable compensation | Rule 45(d)(3)(B)(ii) |

### **J. Pre-Filing Investigation and Experts** {#j.-pre-filing-investigation-and-experts}

* Rule 11 requires "reasonable inquiry" before filing  
* Presumes informal investigation — potentially including expert consultation — before litigation begins

## **PART III: SCOPE OF DISCOVERY — SUMMARY OF BASIC PRINCIPLES** {#part-iii:-scope-of-discovery-—-summary-of-basic-principles}

### **A. Pre-Filing Obligations: Informal Investigation** {#a.-pre-filing-obligations:-informal-investigation}

1. **Rule 11 Reasonable Inquiry**: Lawyer must conduct informal investigation before filing  
2. **Ethical Constraints**:  
   * Cannot contact **represented persons** without consent of their lawyer (may extend to corporate agents/employees)  
   * With **unrepresented persons**: must identify self clearly, avoid misunderstandings, refrain from deceit (including secret recording in many jurisdictions)

### **B. General Scope of Discovery** {#b.-general-scope-of-discovery}

1. **Broad Standard**: Any nonprivileged matter **relevant** to a claim or defense and **proportional** to the needs of the case  
2. Discoverable matter **need not be admissible** at trial  
3. Substantive evidentiary objections (other than privilege) are generally **unavailable** to resist discovery  
4. **Proportionality** may limit discovery; burden on the **resisting party** to show disproportionality

### **C. Privilege** {#c.-privilege}

1. **Definition**: Protected from both discovery and admissibility by an evidentiary privilege  
2. **Sources**: Common law, statutes, rules of evidence  
3. **Purpose**: Protect confidentiality of communications in socially favored relationships (lawyer-client, physician-patient, priest-penitent)  
4. Civil procedure rules **do not create** privileges; Rule 26(b)(1) merely **recognizes** them

### **D. Work Product Protection** {#d.-work-product-protection}

1. **Definition**: Documents and tangible things prepared on behalf of a client **in anticipation of litigation or trial**  
2. **Qualified protection** under Rule 26(b)(3) — protects the lawyer's **value added**, not the underlying facts  
3. **Overcoming**: Discovering party must show:  
   * (a) **Substantial need** to prove case or defense in chief  
   * (b) **Undue hardship** in obtaining the equivalent by other means  
4. **Timing**: Litigation need not have begun; sufficient if product was primarily prepared with a specific claim in mind, to provide legal advice about possible claims, or for the primary purpose of litigation (varies by circuit)

### **E. Expert Discovery Summary** {#e.-expert-discovery-summary}

1. **Testifying experts**: Opinions and facts (acquired as expert) are fully discoverable  
2. **Non-testifying but retained**: Discoverable only on showing of impracticability of obtaining equivalent by other means  
3. **Consulted but not retained/employed**: Rules impliedly permit **no discovery** (by omission)  
4. **Fact witness experts (percipient)**: Discovery available as from any ordinary fact witness  
5. **Critical takeaway**: Precise identification of expert category is essential

## **PART IV: DISCOVERY TOOLS** {#part-iv:-discovery-tools}

### **A. Mandatory Initial Disclosures (Rule 26(a)(1); 26(e); 37(c)(1))** {#a.-mandatory-initial-disclosures-(rule-26(a)(1);-26(e);-37(c)(1))}

### **1\. Two Stages of Discovery** {#1.-two-stages-of-discovery}

* **Stage One**: Required disclosures (Rule 26(a)) — automatic exchange of basic information  
* **Stage Two**: Discretionary discovery — depositions, interrogatories, document requests, physical/mental exams, requests for admissions

### **2\. Scope of Required Initial Disclosures** {#2.-scope-of-required-initial-disclosures}

* **Narrower than general discovery**: Only information the disclosing party **may use to support its own claims or defenses**  
* Not the same as all relevant evidence  
* Practical significance: Defendant need only disclose witnesses/documents it intends to use for **its** defenses — far smaller universe than everything the plaintiff might want

### **3\. Timing and Scheduling** {#3.-timing-and-scheduling}

* Rule 26(f) meet-and-confer: At least **21 days before** scheduling conference/order deadline  
* Rule 16(b) scheduling order: Within **90 days** after any defendant served or **60 days** after any defendant appeared  
* Initial disclosures: Within **14 days** after Rule 26(f) conference

### **4\. Mechanisms to Delay or Avoid Initial Disclosures** {#4.-mechanisms-to-delay-or-avoid-initial-disclosures}

* **(a) Stipulation** with opposing party (Rule 26(a)(1)(C))  
* **(b) Object during discovery conference** and state objection in proposed discovery plan (Rule 26(a)(1)(C))  
* **(c) Move for court order** postponing disclosure (Rule 26(a)(1)(C))  
* **Self-help non-disclosure is NOT authorized**

### **5\. Limitations of Required Disclosures** {#5.-limitations-of-required-disclosures}

* A party is **not required** to disclose witnesses whose testimony would **damage** its own case  
* Discovering party should still pursue **discretionary discovery** (e.g., interrogatories) to uncover unfavorable information the disclosing party has no obligation to volunteer

### **6\. Other Required Disclosures** {#6.-other-required-disclosures}

* **Expert witness disclosures** (Rule 26(a)(2)): Due at least 90 days before trial  
* **Pretrial disclosures** (Rule 26(a)(3)): Due at least 30 days before trial — witness lists, exhibit lists

### **7\. Sanctions for Failure to Disclose (Rule 37(c)(1))** {#7.-sanctions-for-failure-to-disclose-(rule-37(c)(1))}

* **Self-executing**: Undisclosed evidence/witness excluded from use — no motion required  
* Exception: Failure was **substantially justified or harmless**  
* Additional court-imposed sanctions available on motion

### **B. Interrogatories (Rule 33; Rule 26(e))** {#b.-interrogatories-(rule-33;-rule-26(e))}

### **1\. Basic Procedure** {#1.-basic-procedure}

* Up to **25 written interrogatories** (including discrete subparts) served on a **party only** (not nonparties)  
* Response due within **30 days**  
* Answers: in writing, **under oath**, signed by the answering person  
* Objections: in writing, signed by the **attorney**, stated with **specificity**  
* Lawyer must also certify under **Rule 26(g)** (reasonable inquiry)  
* **Pros**: Relatively inexpensive  
* **Cons**: Lawyer-drafted answers tend to be bare and unhelpful

### **2\. Scope of Answering Obligation** {#2.-scope-of-answering-obligation}

* Corporate parties must furnish information **available to the party** (Rule 33(b)(1)(B))  
* Includes information known to employees, agents, and lawyers  
* Must conduct **reasonable inquiry** (interviewing employees, reviewing documents)

### **3\. Option to Produce Business Records (Rule 33(d))** {#3.-option-to-produce-business-records-(rule-33(d))}

* If answers derivable from business records and burden is **substantially the same** for either party  
* Responding party specifies records; gives reasonable opportunity to examine  
* **Practical concern**: Rarely exercised if files contain privileged or sensitive materials

### **4\. Objections to Interrogatories** {#4.-objections-to-interrogatories}

* **(a) Boilerplate "general objections"**: Increasingly **disfavored by courts**; may be stricken or sanctioned; must satisfy specificity requirement  
* **(b) Burdensome interrogatories**: 25-interrogatory limit addresses this; additional interrogatories require agreement or leave of court  
* **(c) Overbroad individual interrogatories**: Court may **narrow** the interrogatory (e.g., limiting time period, excluding production-line employees); parties should resolve in Rule 26(f) meet-and-confer  
* **(d) Pure legal conclusions vs. contention interrogatories**:  
  * **Pure legal conclusion** (e.g., "Is assumption of the risk a defense in Old Jersey?"): **Objectionable** — seeks a legal essay unrelated to case facts  
  * **Contention interrogatory** (e.g., "Do you contend plaintiff assumed the risk?"): **Not objectionable** — asks how law applies to facts of this case; court may defer answer until discovery is complete

### **5\. Duty to Supplement (Rule 26(e))** {#5.-duty-to-supplement-(rule-26(e))}

* Must supplement if response becomes **materially incomplete or incorrect**  
* Only if corrective information **not already otherwise known** to other parties  
* A discovering party's instructions (e.g., "continuing interrogatories" preamble) **cannot impose obligations beyond the Rules**  
* **Best practice**: Supplement even when not strictly required

### **C. Requests for Production of Documents and Things (Rules 34, 45\)** {#c.-requests-for-production-of-documents-and-things-(rules-34,-45)}

### **1\. Procedure (Rule 34\)** {#1.-procedure-(rule-34)}

* Describe documents/categories with **reasonable particularity**  
* Response due within **30 days**  
* Responding party must comply or object in writing  
* Documents already received through initial disclosures reduce the need for extensive requests

### **2\. Scope: "Possession, Custody, or Control"** {#2.-scope:-"possession,-custody,-or-control"}

* Includes documents held by **agents** (lawyers, accountants, banks)  
* **Improper tactics**:  
  * Giving documents to lawyer to hide — still in party's "control"  
  * Shredding — spoliation; potential sanctions and criminal liability  
  * Burying among irrelevant documents — violates Rule 34(b) production requirements  
  * Silent withholding as work product — must expressly state objection with description (Rule 26(b)(5))  
  * Narrow construction to exclude responsive documents — risks credibility damage; violates Rule 26(g)(1) certification

### **3\. Nonparties (Rule 34(c))** {#3.-nonparties-(rule-34(c))}

* Compelled via **Rule 45 subpoena**

### **D. Electronically Stored Information (ESI) and E-Discovery** {#d.-electronically-stored-information-(esi)-and-e-discovery}

### **1\. The Rise of ESI** {#1.-the-rise-of-esi}

* By 1999, 93% of all information generated digitally; 30% never printed  
* Pre-2006 Rules inadequate for ESI

### **2\. Key E-Discovery Rules and Principles** {#2.-key-e-discovery-rules-and-principles}

* **(a) Accessibility limitation** (Rule 26(b)(2)(B)): No duty to produce from sources **not reasonably accessible** due to undue burden/cost (burden on responding party to show)  
* **(b) Cost allocation**: Presumption that responding party pays; costs may be shifted or limited under Rule 26(c)(1)(B)  
* **(c) Litigation holds** (*Zubulake IV & V*): Once litigation reasonably anticipated, must suspend routine destruction policies and implement a litigation hold; counsel must oversee compliance

### **3\. E-Discovery Roadmap** {#3.-e-discovery-roadmap}

* (a) Identify client's ESI  
* (b) Preserve via litigation hold  
* (c) Meet and confer on e-discovery plan (Rule 26(f)(3)(C))  
* (d) Process and convert ESI  
* (e) Search and analyze  
* (f) Disclose; refine and rerun searches as needed  
* (g) Engage e-discovery specialists as needed

### **4\. Predictive Coding / Technology-Assisted Review (TAR)** {#4.-predictive-coding-/-technology-assisted-review-(tar)}

* Algorithms learn relevance from human-coded "seed set" through iterative process  
* Requires human review of only \~**1.9%** of documents (fifty-fold savings)  
* **Widely accepted** — considered "black letter law"  
* Keyword-agnostic

### **5\. Attorney Competence Obligations** {#5.-attorney-competence-obligations}

* Must be able to (or associate with experts to): assess e-discovery needs, implement preservation, understand client's ESI systems, advise on collection, identify custodians, engage in competent meet-and-confer, perform searches, collect ESI preserving integrity, produce in recognized manner

### **6\. Waskul v. Washtenaw County (E.D. Mich. 2021\) — Key Lessons** {#6.-waskul-v.-washtenaw-county-(e.d.-mich.-2021)-—-key-lessons}

* Attorneys must understand basic e-discovery terminology  
* Must interview custodians early  
* Must not abdicate responsibility for designing proportional searches  
* Must engage specialists when out of depth  
* Must set deadlines for production  
* Proportionality arguments require reference to **Rule 26(b)(1) factors** and substantiation with evidence

### **E. Depositions (Rules 30, 31, 45\)** {#e.-depositions-(rules-30,-31,-45)}

### **1\. Procedure for Obtaining a Deposition** {#1.-procedure-for-obtaining-a-deposition}

* **(a) Noticing**: Serve notice of time, place, and recording method on party-deponent; copies to all parties  
* **(b) Non-party deponents**: Require a **subpoena** under Rule 45; compliance within **100 miles** of residence/employment/regular business  
* **(c) Organizational depositions** (Rule 30(b)(6)): Describe matters for examination; organization designates knowledgeable witness(es); parties confer in good faith; designee's testimony admissible against the organization

### **2\. Conduct of Depositions** {#2.-conduct-of-depositions}

* Oral examination under oath, outside presence of judge, recorded by court reporter  
* No judge present — objections noted on record; testimony continues subject to objection  
* **Duration**: 1 day of 7 hours (default)

### **3\. Handling Objections** {#3.-handling-objections}

| Type | When Made | Waived if Not Made? |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Substantive evidentiary (relevance, competency) | Preserved until trial | No — Rule 32(d)(3)(A) |
| Form objections (compound, ambiguous, leading) | Must be made at deposition | Yes — Rule 32(d)(3)(B) |
| Privilege | Must be made at deposition | Yes — answering waives; may instruct not to answer |

### **4\. Privilege at Depositions** {#4.-privilege-at-depositions}

* Answering after privilege objection **effectively waives** the privilege  
* Lawyer may **instruct client not to answer** on privilege grounds  
* Discovering party must file **motion to compel** if it wants an answer

### **5\. Lawyer Conduct** {#5.-lawyer-conduct}

* Objections must be **concise, nonargumentative, nonsuggestive** (Rule 30(d)(1))  
* **Coaching through objections** prohibited  
* Courts may sanction obstruction and incivility

### **6\. Types of Depositions by Purpose** {#6.-types-of-depositions-by-purpose}

* **Discovery deposition**: Preview and impeachment; open-ended questioning; witness expected to testify live at trial  
* **De bene esse deposition**: Substitute for live testimony when witness will be unavailable; more guarded questioning

### **7\. Using Depositions at Trial — Two-Layer Analysis (Rule 32\)** {#7.-using-depositions-at-trial-—-two-layer-analysis-(rule-32)}

* **Layer 1 ("The Box")**: Does the deposition satisfy Rule 32(a)?  
  * Usable against any party represented at or with notice of the deposition  
  * Permitted uses: impeachment, admission by party-deponent/officer/director/managing agent/designee, witness unavailability  
* **Layer 2 ("The Contents")**: Does specific testimony satisfy the **Federal Rules of Evidence**?  
  * Individual questions/answers must independently satisfy

### **F. Physical and Mental Examinations (Rule 35\)** {#f.-physical-and-mental-examinations-(rule-35)}

### **1\. Method for Obtaining** {#1.-method-for-obtaining}

* Requires a **court order** absent the examinee's consent  
* Movant must show:  
  * (a) The person's **condition is in controversy**  
  * (b) **Good cause** exists for the examination  
* Court order must specify **scope** of the examination  
* In most personal injury cases, plaintiffs will **stipulate** to an examination without court involvement

### **2\. Condition in Controversy and Good Cause** {#2.-condition-in-controversy-and-good-cause}

* **(a) Mental examinations**: Must show mental state is genuinely in controversy  
  * Merely conclusory pleading allegations are **not necessarily sufficient** (*Schlagenhauf v. Holder*, 379 U.S. 104 (1964))  
  * May need affidavits or other materials  
  * Unless plaintiff seeks damages for **mental distress or injury**, complaint likely does not put mental condition in controversy  
* **(b) Good cause**: Even if condition is in controversy, must separately show good cause  
  * Gives court additional basis for denying examinations  
  * Example: No good cause for blood tests already performed

### **3\. Who May Be Examined** {#3.-who-may-be-examined}

* Limited to **parties** or **persons in the custody or legal control of a party**  
* Example: Spouse in a loss-of-consortium claim

### **4\. Examiner's Report (Rule 35(b))** {#4.-examiner's-report-(rule-35(b))}

* Moving party must deliver report on request  
* Report must include detailed findings, diagnoses, conclusions, test results  
* **Waiver of privilege**: Requesting/obtaining the report or deposing the examiner waives privilege concerning **all examinations of the same condition** in that action or any other action involving the same controversy

### **G. Requests for Admissions (Rule 36\)** {#g.-requests-for-admissions-(rule-36)}

### **1\. Scope** {#1.-scope}

* May request admission of:  
  * (a) Facts, application of law to fact, or opinions about either  
  * (b) Genuineness of described documents  
* For purposes of the **pending action only**

### **2\. Procedure** {#2.-procedure}

* Each matter stated **separately**  
* Request to admit genuineness of a document must be accompanied by a **copy** (unless already furnished)

### **3\. Timing and Effect of Non-Response** {#3.-timing-and-effect-of-non-response}

* **Deemed admitted** if no written answer or objection served within **30 days**  
* This is a powerful consequence — failure to respond results in **conclusive establishment** of the matter

### **4\. Answering Requirements** {#4.-answering-requirements}

* Must **specifically deny** or state in detail why the party cannot truthfully admit or deny  
* Denial must **fairly respond** to the substance  
* May qualify an answer or deny only part  
* May assert **lack of knowledge or information** only if the party states it has made **reasonable inquiry** and information known or readily obtainable is insufficient

### **5\. Objections** {#5.-objections}

* Grounds must be stated  
* **Cannot object solely** on the ground that the request presents a genuine issue for trial  
* Court may determine sufficiency of answer/objection on motion (Rule 36(a)(6))

### **6\. Effect of Admissions (Rule 36(b))** {#6.-effect-of-admissions-(rule-36(b))}

* **Conclusively established** in the pending action  
* Withdrawal/amendment permitted only if:  
  * (a) It would **promote presentation of the merits**, and  
  * (b) Court is **not persuaded** it would prejudice the requesting party  
* An admission under Rule 36 is **not an admission for any other purpose** and **cannot be used in any other proceeding**

### **7\. Strategic Use** {#7.-strategic-use}

* Typically used **after other discovery** to:  
  * Authenticate documents  
  * Narrow issues for trial  
  * Eliminate the need to prove uncontested facts

## **PART V: STRATEGIC SEQUENCING OF DISCOVERY** {#part-v:-strategic-sequencing-of-discovery}

### **A. General Principle** {#a.-general-principle-1}

* Depositions of key witnesses should typically come **last, not first**  
* A party needs documents and information to prepare effective deposition questions

### **B. Common Discovery Sequence** {#b.-common-discovery-sequence}

1. **Required initial disclosures**  
2. **Interrogatories** — to locate and identify evidence  
3. **Requests for document production** — to collect written evidence and ESI  
4. **Depositions** — armed with documents and information; key witnesses last

### **C. More Detailed Suggested Sequence (Zweifach)** {#c.-more-detailed-suggested-sequence-(zweifach)}

1. Required disclosures  
2. Depose secondary witnesses  
3. Follow-up requests for production and interrogatories  
4. Depose key witnesses  
5. Depose the adverse party  
6. Follow-up requests to admit  
7. Depose expert witnesses  
8. Contention interrogatories  
9. Final "clean up" discovery requests

### **D. Additional Tools in the Sequence** {#d.-additional-tools-in-the-sequence}

* **Medical examinations**: Commonly stipulated when physical/mental conditions are in controversy  
* **Requests for admissions**: Used after other discovery to authenticate documents and narrow trial issues

## **PART VI: DISCOVERY TOOLS — SUMMARY OF BASIC PRINCIPLES** {#part-vi:-discovery-tools-—-summary-of-basic-principles}

1. Parties must **meet early** to discuss discovery issues and propose a discovery plan  
2. Initial disclosures required in all but a small number of cases — limited to information the disclosing party **may use to support its own claims or defenses** (not information helpful to the other side; not impeachment-only information)  
3. Beyond required disclosures, all other discovery is **discretionary and party-driven**  
   * Interrogatories and document requests: **parties only**  
   * Depositions: **parties and nonparties**  
   * Discovery fruits may be admissible and may constitute admissions, but are **not conclusive** — except **admissions under Rule 36**, which are conclusive in the action  
4. Interrogatories must be answered with **all information available to the party**; "empty head" answers are rarely proper, especially for institutional parties  
5. Document requests target items in the responding party's **possession, custody, or control** — cannot be avoided by transferring to an agent (including the party's lawyer)  
6. **E-discovery** poses unique proportionality issues due to volume, variable accessibility, use of predictive coding/TAR, and resulting expenses; special rules address limiting, responding to, and producing ESI  
7. **Privilege and work product** are proper objections but must be **expressly stated with a proper foundation** — no "silent objections" under the Rules  
8. **Nonparty depositions** require a **subpoena** under Rule 45; organizational depositions under **Rule 30(b)(6)** require designation of a knowledgeable witness whose testimony is admissible against the organization  
9. **Physical/mental examinations** require a **court order** due to privacy concerns — available only for conditions in controversy and on a showing of good cause

# **WEEK 6: BROAD DISCOVERY HAS ITS LIMITS** {#week-6:-broad-discovery-has-its-limits}

# **I. Governing Framework: Scope & Limits on Discovery** {#i.-governing-framework:-scope-&-limits-on-discovery}

## **A. Rule 26(b)(1): What Is Discoverable** {#a.-rule-26(b)(1):-what-is-discoverable}

Information is discoverable if it meets all three requirements:

**1\. Not privileged**

**2\. Relevant to a claim or defense**

**3\. Proportional to the needs of the case** (applying listed proportionality factors)

## **B. Key Exceptions / “Stops” to Broad Discovery** {#b.-key-exceptions-/-“stops”-to-broad-discovery}

* **Privilege** (26(b)(1)) — not discoverable  
* **Irrelevance** (26(b)(1)) — not discoverable  
* **Not proportional** (26(b)(1)) — not discoverable  
* **Cumulative/duplicative** — better source available or ample earlier opportunity (26(b)(2)(C))  
* **Work product / trial prep materials** (26(b)(3))  
* **Non-testifying expert limits** (26(a)(2), 26(b)(4))  
* **Protective orders** can restrict discovery (26(c))

## **C. Discovery Tools Have Presumptive Limits** {#c.-discovery-tools-have-presumptive-limits}

Depositions, document requests, interrogatories, requests to admit, and exams all have built-in caps and procedural requirements.

# **II. Motions, Enforcement & Sanctions** {#ii.-motions,-enforcement-&-sanctions}

## **A. Motions** {#a.-motions}

* **Motion to Compel:** Rule 37(a)  
* **Motion for Protective Order:** Rule 26(c)

## **B. Sanctions** {#b.-sanctions}

* **Rule 37** — discovery failures (includes 37(c) for nondisclosure; 37(e) for ESI spoliation)  
* **Rule 26(g)** — certification/signing violations  
* **Spoliation:** common law \+ Rule 37(e) for ESI

# **III. Practical Discovery Policies (Cost & Access)** {#iii.-practical-discovery-policies-(cost-&-access)}

**If you have it and it’s easy to produce, you must give it.** Example: a police report already in your possession.

**If it’s cheaper/easier for the requesting party to obtain independently,** the court may expect them to get it themselves (ties to proportionality).

# **IV. Privileges (Discovery “Full Stop”)** {#iv.-privileges-(discovery-“full-stop”)}

## **A. Core Idea** {#a.-core-idea}

If information is **privileged**, discovery ends. ***Relevance and proportionality do not override a valid privilege.***

## **B. Common Privileges** {#b.-common-privileges}

* **Attorney–client privilege**  
* **Work product doctrine** (26(b)(3)) — related but analytically distinct  
* **Spousal privilege**  
* **Doctor–patient privilege**  
* **Fifth Amendment** privilege against self-incrimination  
* **Clergy–penitent privilege**

# **V. Work Product Doctrine (Rule 26(b)(3))** {#v.-work-product-doctrine-(rule-26(b)(3))}

## **A. Definition** {#a.-definition-1}

Materials created for litigation/trial preparation (memos, videos, witness statements, etc.).

## **B. Key Classification Question** {#b.-key-classification-question}

Was it created ***because of litigation*** or is it a ***routine business record***?

## **C. Can Work Product Still Be Discovered?** {#c.-can-work-product-still-be-discovered?}

Yes, if the requesting party demonstrates:

**1\. Substantial need**

**2\. Undue hardship/burden/expense** to obtain the equivalent by other means

***Note:** Protections remain stronger for opinion work product.*

# **VI. Disclosure Duties & Supplementing** {#vi.-disclosure-duties-&-supplementing}

## **A. Rule 26(f): Discovery Conference (“Meet and Confer”)** {#a.-rule-26(f):-discovery-conference-(“meet-and-confer”)}

**Timing:** At least 21 days before the Rule 16(b) scheduling conference/order.

**Purpose:** Create a discovery plan addressing topics listed in 26(f)(2)–(3).

## **B. Rule 26(d): Timing/Sequence** {#b.-rule-26(d):-timing/sequence}

Discovery generally begins after the 26(f) conference unless otherwise authorized.

## **C. Rule 26(e): Duty to Supplement** {#c.-rule-26(e):-duty-to-supplement}

Must supplement if responses/disclosures become **incomplete or incorrect**.

Must do so timely, unless info already “made known.”

## **D. Required Disclosures (Rule 26(a))** {#d.-required-disclosures-(rule-26(a))}

* **Initial disclosures** (26(a)(1))  
* **Expert disclosures** (26(a)(2))  
* **Pretrial disclosures** (26(a)(3))  
* **Nondisclosure sanctions:** Rule 37(c)

# **VII. Rule 26(g): Signing & Certification (“Discovery’s Rule 11”)** {#vii.-rule-26(g):-signing-&-certification-(“discovery’s-rule-11”)}

## **A. Signature Requirement** {#a.-signature-requirement}

Must sign all disclosures, discovery requests, responses, and objections.

## **B. What the Signature Certifies** {#b.-what-the-signature-certifies}

**For Disclosures:**

Complete and correct.

**For Requests/Responses/Objections:**

* Consistent with rules and law (or nonfrivolous argument for change)  
* Not for improper purpose (harass / delay / increase cost)  
* Not unreasonable or unduly burdensome

## **C. Failure to Sign** {#c.-failure-to-sign}

No response required from opposing party. Court must strike unless promptly corrected.

## **D. Sanctions** {#d.-sanctions}

Court ***must*** impose appropriate sanctions (can include attorneys’ fees).

# **IX. Interrogatories — Rule 33** {#ix.-interrogatories-—-rule-33}

## **A. Basics** {#a.-basics}

* Served on **parties only**  
* **25 maximum** without leave or court agreement  
* Must answer/object within **30 days**  
* Answers **under oath** (signed by party); objections signed by attorney  
* If objecting, must still answer **to extent not objectionable**  
* Duty to supplement (26(e))

## **B. Permissible Scope** {#b.-permissible-scope}

Facts, opinions, and contentions (application of law to fact is allowed).

## **C. Rule 33(d): Business Records Option** {#c.-rule-33(d):-business-records-option}

If the answer can be derived from business records, the responding party may produce/specify them (shifts the burden of finding the answer).

## **D. Good Uses** {#d.-good-uses}

Identify witnesses, facts behind vague claims/defenses, documents/things and their location, damages calculations, timelines, parties/agents/employees, and who prepared interrogatory answers. Useful early in litigation, but limited because there are no follow-up questions.

# **X. Requests for Production — Rule 34** {#x.-requests-for-production-—-rule-34}

## **A. Basics** {#a.-basics-1}

* Served on **parties only** (nonparties via Rule 45 subpoena)  
* **No numerical limit**  
* Must describe items/categories with **reasonable particularity**  
* Must specify time/place/manner and **ESI format** if desired  
* Response due in **30 days** in writing

## **B. Objections/Withholding** {#b.-objections/withholding}

* Must state objections **with specificity**, including reasons  
* Must indicate what is being withheld (34(b)(2)(C))  
* Privilege/work product objections require compliance with **26(b)(5)(A)**

## **C. Good vs. Bad Requests** {#c.-good-vs.-bad-requests}

**Good:** Specific time frames, transactions, events, contention-targeted documents.

**Avoid:** “All documents relevant to the dispute” — too broad (proportionality \+ particularity problems).

# **XI. ESI Rules & Responsibilities** {#xi.-esi-rules-&-responsibilities}

## **A. Rule 26(b)(2)(B): Not Reasonably Accessible ESI** {#a.-rule-26(b)(2)(b):-not-reasonably-accessible-esi}

* Responding party must show **undue burden/cost**  
* Court may still order production on **good cause**, applying 26(b)(2)(C)  
* Court may set conditions, including **cost shifting**

## **B. Litigation Holds / Pre-Suit Duties** {#b.-litigation-holds-/-pre-suit-duties}

ESI obligations may begin **before filing** (preservation when litigation is reasonably anticipated).

## **C. Predictive Coding** {#c.-predictive-coding}

Recognize as a tool/issue in ESI search methodologies.

## **D. Counsel Competence** {#d.-counsel-competence}

Counsel must be competent in e-discovery or associate with someone who is (reinforced by Waskul).

## **E. Waskul v. Washtenaw County CMH — Key Takeaways** {#e.-waskul-v.-washtenaw-county-cmh-—-key-takeaways}

* Responding party bears responsibility for ESI search methodology  
* Boilerplate objections are ineffective/waived  
* Resource limits / technical ignorance not a valid excuse  
* Proportionality and cooperation emphasized

# **XII. Depositions — Rule 30** {#xii.-depositions-—-rule-30}

## **A. Basics** {#a.-basics-2}

* Parties and nonparties (nonparties require **Rule 45 subpoena**)  
* **10-deposition limit** without leave (30(a)(2))  
* **1 day / 7 hours** presumptive limit (30(d)(1))  
* 30(b)(6) organizational witness notice

## **B. Objections/Refusals** {#b.-objections/refusals}

Must make objections, but refusal to answer is limited to:

* Privilege  
* Court order  
* Motion to terminate / seek immediate order (30(c)(2), 30(d)(3))

## **C. Misconduct Controls** {#c.-misconduct-controls}

* Sanctions for impeding (30(d)(2))  
* Motion to terminate/limit for bad faith or harassment (30(d)(3))

## **D. Uses** {#d.-uses}

Candid testimony with follow-up questioning. Downside: expensive.

# **XIII. Mental & Physical Exams — Rule 35** {#xiii.-mental-&-physical-exams-—-rule-35}

## **A. Requirements** {#a.-requirements}

* **Parties only**  
* Must file a **motion**  
* Must show **good cause**  
* Condition must be **in controversy**  
* Court order specifies time, place, manner, and conditions

## **B. Reports** {#b.-reports}

If requested, the examiner’s report must be provided; then reciprocal reports may be requested.

## **C. Use** {#c.-use}

Neutral information about mental/physical condition (common in personal injury cases).

# **XIV. Requests to Admit — Rule 36** {#xiv.-requests-to-admit-—-rule-36}

## **A. Basics** {#a.-basics-3}

* Parties only  
* Matters **deemed admitted** unless responded to within **30 days**  
* Responses: admit, deny (properly), or object

## **B. Effect** {#b.-effect}

Admission is **conclusively established for this case only**

Can withdraw/amend with court permission (36(b)).

## **C. Best Uses** {#c.-best-uses}

* Undisputed facts  
* Opinions as to facts  
* **Genuineness of documents** (most common)  
* Application of law to fact (not pure legal propositions)

**I. Limits on Broad Discovery — Rule 26(b)(1)**

* A. Discoverability standard: not privileged, relevant to claim/defense, proportionate to needs  
* B. Exceptions to discoverability

* D. Sanctions: Rule 37, Rule 26(g), spoliation (common law & Rule 37(e))

**II. Reading the Discovery Rules**

* A. Rule 26 — General discovery procedures (disclosures, scope/limits, work product, expert discovery, protective orders, duty to supplement, party conference, certification under 26(g))  
* B. Rules 27–36 — Discovery tools overview (nature of tool, who it reaches, limits, response requirements)

**III.** ***Chudasama v. Mazda Motor Corp.***

* A. Mazda's four discovery strategies: blanket objections, 12(b)(6) motion, protective order, withholding discovery  
* B. Eleventh Circuit holdings  
  * Compel order was abuse of discretion (failure to rule on MTD and objections)  
  * Sanctions were excessive (default judgment disproportionate; required bad faith finding; 26(g) sanctions only "last resort")  
* C. Reassignment to different judge  
* D. Key takeaways: shared responsibility (Rule 26(g) certification, judicial case management, proportionality, graduated sanctions, resolving pretrial motions before compelling broad discovery)  
* E. Importance of 12(b)(6) — narrow issues before discovery

**IV. Discovery Controls**

* A. Rule 26(g) — Certification of discovery papers  
* B. Rule 37(b)–(d) — Discovery sanctions  
* C. Protective orders — Rule 26(c)  
* D. Sequence and timing — Rule 26(d), 26(c), 12(b)(6), 56(a)  
* E. Proportionality — Rule 26(b)(1) (six factors)  
* F. ESI-specific limits — Rule 26(b)(2)(B)

**V. Spoliation**

* A. Definition: destruction/alteration of evidence or failure to preserve  
* B. Three elements for sanctions: obligation, culpable state of mind, relevance  
* C. Range of sanctions (adverse inference → default judgment)  
* D. Common law vs. Rule 37(e) for ESI  
  * Common law: negligence can trigger sanctions (including adverse inference)  
  * Rule 37(e): severe sanctions require *intentional* destruction; more lenient for ESI given difficulty of preservation

**VI. Discovery Process Flow**

* Phase 0: Informal investigation  
* Phase 1: Meet and confer (Rule 26(f))  
* Phase 2: Mandatory initial disclosures (Rule 26(a)) — witnesses, documents, damages, insurance  
* Phase 3: Party-driven discovery (Rules 30–36) — interrogatories, document requests, depositions, physical/mental exams, requests for admission  
* Phase 4: Expert disclosures (Rule 26(a)(2))  
* Phase 5: Pretrial disclosures (Rule 26(a)(3))  
* Throughout: Disputes, objections, protective orders, motions to compel, sanctions

**VII. Rule 16 — Pretrial Case Management**

* A. Purposes (16(a)): expediting, establishing control, discouraging waste, improving trial quality, facilitating settlement  
* B. Scheduling (16(b)): timing, required/permitted contents, "good cause" modification standard  
* C. Attendance and matters for consideration (16(c))  
  * Attendance: attorney must have authority to act; parties available for settlement discussions  
  * Critical distinction: authority to discuss ≠ obligation to agree  
  * Seven categories of matters: narrowing issues, managing discovery, resolving legal issues, trial preparation, settlement/ADR, special case management, catch-all  
* D. Pretrial orders (16(d)): controls the action unless modified  
* E. Final pretrial order (16(e)): supersedes pleadings; "manifest injustice" modification standard; unspoken exclusionary rule  
* F. Sanctions (16(f)): failure to appear, unpreparedness, disobedience

**VIII.** ***J.F. Edwards Construction Co. v. Anderson Safeway Guard Rail Corp.***

* A. Seventh Circuit vacated all sanctions  
* B. Rule 16 does not authorize compelled stipulations  
* C. Standing orders cannot exceed Rule 16's authority (Rule 83\)  
* D. Sanctions not justified — Anderson wasn't dilatory  
* E. Pretrial is informational, not coercive (Judge Clark: not "to club the parties into submission")

**IX.** ***Davey v. Lockheed Martin Corp.***

* A. Framework for Rule 16(e) modification — four-factor test: prejudice/surprise, ability to cure, disruption, bad faith  
* B. Connection to *Edwards*: courts can exclude issues via pretrial order, but must balance fairness, proportionality, and right to present case

    │    Defendant on counterclaim)

    │

    └── Non-Movant has BURDEN OF PROOF at trial

        (e.g., Defendant against plaintiff's claim)

---

STEP 2: WHAT IS THE MOVANT'S BURDEN?

         ↓

    IF MOVANT HAS BURDEN OF PROOF AT TRIAL:

        ↓

        Movant must affirmatively show

        NO GENUINE DISPUTE on EACH ELEMENT

        of the claim/defense.

        → Must present evidence supporting

          ALL elements.

        → If they fail, SJ is denied.

    IF NON-MOVANT HAS BURDEN OF PROOF AT TRIAL:

        ↓

        Movant does NOT need to prove a negative.

        Per Celotex: Movant can rely on:

        → Pleadings

        → Depositions

        → Answers to interrogatories

        → Admissions

        → Affidavits (if proper)

        → Etc.

        to show NON-MOVANT LACKS EVIDENCE

        on at least ONE dispositive element.

        → This is a "naked" or "show me" motion.

---

STEP 3: HAS THE MOVANT MET ITS BURDEN?

         ↓

    If YES → Burden SHIFTS to Non-Movant.

    If NO → SJ is DENIED.

---

STEP 4: THE NON-MOVANT'S BURDEN (IF SHIFTED)

         ↓

    Non-movant must go BEYOND the pleadings.

    Must designate SPECIFIC FACTS showing

    a GENUINE DISPUTE for trial.

    Must use:

    ├── Their OWN affidavits/declarations

    ├── PLUS evidence from the record

        (depositions, interrogatories,

         admissions, etc.)

         ↓

    CANNOT rely solely on:

    ├── Allegations in their own pleading

    │   (unsworn, not personal knowledge)

    ├── Mere "naked assertions"

    ├── Hoping to create issues through

    │   cross-examination at trial

    │   (Slaven)

    └── Simply saying "I can prove it"

        (Defendant's affidavit example)

---

STEP 5: WHAT CONSTITUTES THE RECORD?            \[Rule 56(c)(1)(A)\]

         ↓

    Must be EVIDENCE that would be ADMISSIBLE

    at trial. Can include:

    ├── Depositions (properly authenticated)

    ├── Affidavits/Declarations (must meet 56(c)(4) standard)

    ├── Documents (invoices, contracts, etc.)

    ├── Stipulations (parties agree facts are undisputed)

    ├── Admissions

    ├── Interrogatory answers

    ├── Etc.

         ↓

     OBJECTION: Material cited cannot be

     presented in admissible form.            \[Rule 56(c)(2)\]

---

STEP 6: REQUIREMENTS FOR AFFIDAVITS/DECLARATIONS \[Rule 56(c)(4)\]

         ↓

    MUST BE:

    ├── 1\. Made on PERSONAL KNOWLEDGE

    │   → Affiant must be "eyeball witness" /

    │     percipient witness (attorney's affidavit

    │     from client is hearsay/not personal)

    │

    ├── 2\. Set out FACTS that would be

    │       ADMISSIBLE IN EVIDENCE

    │   → Not conclusory statements

    │   → Must describe specific facts

    │     (e.g., "I saw the light turn red...")

    │   → Not hearsay

    │

    └── 3\. Show affiant is COMPETENT to testify

---

STEP 7: WHAT THE COURT MUST \*NOT\* DO

         ↓

    The Court:

    ├── MUST DETERMINE if a genuine dispute EXISTS

    ├── MUST NOT RESOLVE the dispute

    ├── MUST NOT WEIGH the evidence

    ├── MUST NOT ASSESS credibility

    ├── MUST NOT MAKE FACTUAL DETERMINATIONS

    → It's about the \*existence\* of a dispute,

      not its resolution.

    → The judge is NOT a fact-finder at SJ.

---

STEP 8: TIMING AND PROCEDURES

         ↓

    TIMING:                               \[Rule 56(b)\]

    ├── Motion can be filed anytime until

    │   30 days after close of discovery

    │

    NON-MOVANT'S DILEMMA:                 \[Rule 56(d)\]

    ├── If facts are unavailable to non-movant

    │   for justification, court may:

    │   ├── Defer/Deny motion

    │   ├── Allow time for discovery/affidavits

    │   └── Issue other orders

    │

    MOVANT'S FAILURE TO SUPPORT:           \[Rule 56(e)\]

    ├── If movant fails to properly support

    │   or address a fact, court may:

    │   ├── Give opportunity to fix

    │   ├── Consider fact undisputed

    │   ├── Grant SJ if warranted by undisputed facts

    │   └── Other appropriate orders

    │

    COURT'S OWN MOTION:                   \[Rule 56(f)\]

    ├── Court can grant SJ on grounds

    │   not raised by a party

    ├── Court can grant SJ for a non-movant

    ├── Court can identify undisputed facts

    │   and treat them as established        \[Rule 56(g)\]

    │

    BAD FAITH AFFIDAVITS:                 \[Rule 56(h)\]

    ├── If affidavit submitted in bad faith

    │   or solely for delay, court may

    │   order payment of expenses, or

    │   sanctions (including contempt)

---

### **KEY CASES & CONCEPTS FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT** {#key-cases-&-concepts-for-summary-judgment}

| Case/Concept | Key Takeaway |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Rule 56(a) Standard** | No genuine dispute of material fact \+ Movant entitled to judgment as a matter of law |
| **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby** | Substantive law identifies material facts; only disputes affecting outcome under governing law preclude SJ |
| **Celotex Corp. v. Catrett** | Movant with burden of proof must show lack of evidence on an element; Non-movant must then designate specific facts showing dispute |
| **Palucki v. Sears, Roebuck & Co.** | Judge must ask: would non-moving party have a fair chance of obtaining a verdict at trial? |
| **Slaven v. City of Salem** | Non-movant cannot rely solely on pleading allegations; must provide specific facts, not just bare assertions or hopes of cross-examination |
| **Haley v. [Amazon.com](http://amazon.com/) Services, LLC** | A declaration cannot be dismissed *simply* because it is "self-serving"; credibility/weight are for trial, not SJ |
| **Admissible Evidence** | Evidence cited must be in a form admissible at trial (affidavits must meet 56(c)(4) standards) |
| **Personal Knowledge** | Affiant must have directly perceived the facts |
| **Material Fact** | A fact that could affect the outcome of the case under the governing law |
| **Genuine Dispute** | A real question of fact where reasonable minds could differ, not just a metaphysical possibility |
| **Burden of Production** | Party must produce enough evidence for a rational factfinder to find for them |
| **Burden of Persuasion** | Party must convince the factfinder (preponderance, clear and convincing, etc.) |

# **IV. TRIAL AND POST-TRIAL MOTIONS** {#iv.-trial-and-post-trial-motions}

## **A. Directed Verdict & JNOV (Judgment Notwithstanding the Verdict)** {#a.-directed-verdict-&-jnov-(judgment-notwithstanding-the-verdict)}

---

### **DIRECTED VERDICT \[Rule 50(a)\]** {#directed-verdict-[rule-50(a)]}

DIRECTED VERDICT

         ↓

    When: Motion made by a party

          (usually Defendant)

    Timing: After the opposing party

            has presented its evidence

            (usually Plaintiff's case-in-chief)

    Standard:

    → Court views evidence in light

      most favorable to the non-moving

      party.

    → If NO reasonable jury could find

      for the non-moving party

      (i.e., non-movant failed to meet

      burden of production on an essential

      element), the court GRANTS the motion.

    → EFFECT: Ends the case immediately.

---

### **JUDGMENT NOTWITHSTANDING THE VERDICT (JNOV) \[Rule 50(b)\]** {#judgment-notwithstanding-the-verdict-(jnov)-[rule-50(b)]}

JUDGMENT NOTWITHSTANDING

THE VERDICT (JNOV)

         ↓

    Also known as "Renewed Motion for

    Judgment as a Matter of Law"

         ↓

    Timing: Motion made AFTER the jury

            has returned a verdict.

    Prerequisite: The movant MUST have

                  made a directed verdict

                  motion on the SAME grounds

                  at trial (Rule 50(b))

    Standard:

    → Court views evidence in light

      most favorable to the party

      against whom the verdict was

      returned.

    → If NO reasonable jury could have

      reached the verdict based on the

      evidence presented, the court GRANTS

      the motion.

    → EFFECT: Overturns the jury's verdict.

---

### **HOW THEY RELATE TO SUMMARY JUDGMENT** {#how-they-relate-to-summary-judgment}

* **Directed Verdict/JNOV Standard ≈ Summary Judgment Standard**  
  * Both involve determining if a party has met their burden of production on a claim/defense.  
  * SJ happens BEFORE trial based on written evidence.  
  * DV/JNOV happens DURING/AFTER trial based on evidence presented at trial.  
* **Rule 50(b) Requirement:** You MUST move for a directed verdict at trial to preserve the right to move for JNOV later.

# **V. CONTROLLING THE JURY, NEW TRIAL, AND RELIEF FROM JUDGMENT** {#v.-controlling-the-jury,-new-trial,-and-relief-from-judgment}

## **A. Controlling the Jury** {#a.-controlling-the-jury}

JURY CONTROL MECHANISMS

         ↓

    ├── INSTRUCTIONS \[Rule 51\]

    │   → Judge instructs jury on the law

    │   → Parties submit proposed instructions

    │   → Objections must be made on the record

    │

    ├── INTERROGATORIES \[Rule 49\]

    │   → SPECIAL VERDICT: Jury answers specific

    │     factual questions; judge applies law.

    │   → GENERAL VERDICT WITH INTERROGATORIES:

    │     Jury provides general verdict AND

    │     answers specific factual questions.

    │     → Court checks for consistency. If

    │       inconsistent, court may:

    │       ├── Send back for reconsideration

    │       ├── Enter judgment per general verdict

    │       └── Enter judgment per answers to

    │           interrogatories

    │

    └── IMPEACHMENT OF VERDICT

        → Generally, jurors cannot testify

          about their deliberations to impeach

          their own verdict (FRE 606(b))

        → EXCEPTIONS: For outside influences,

          extraneous prejudicial info, or error

          in the verdict form itself.

## **B. New Trial \[Rule 59\]** {#b.-new-trial-[rule-59]}

MOTION FOR NEW TRIAL

         ↓

    Timing: Must be filed within 28 days

            after entry of judgment

         ↓

    Grounds (for any reason for which a

    new trial has heretofore been granted

    in an action at law in Federal court):

    ├── Errors of law during trial

    ├── Verdict is against the weight of the

    │   evidence (judge may weigh evidence here)

    ├── Excessive or inadequate damages

    ├── Newly discovered evidence that

    │   could not have been discovered

    │   earlier with due diligence

    ├── Juror misconduct (e.g., outside influence)

    └── Improper jury instructions

         ↓

    Standard: For the judge to grant a new

              trial, the error must be

              substantial enough to have

              prejudiced the moving party.

## **C. Relief From Judgment \[Rule 60\]** {#c.-relief-from-judgment-[rule-60]}

MOTION FOR RELIEF FROM JUDGMENT

         ↓

    Timing: Reasonable time; specific time limits

            for some grounds (see Rule 60(c))

         ↓

    Grounds for relief from a FINAL judgment,

    order, or proceeding:

    ├── (1) Mistake, inadvertence, surprise,

    │       or excusable neglect

    ├── (2) Newly discovered evidence

    ├── (3) Fraud, misrepresentation, or

    │       misconduct by an opposing party

    ├── (4) The judgment is void

    ├── (5) The judgment has been satisfied,

    │       released, or discharged; or is

    │       based on an earlier judgment that

    │       has been reversed or vacated; or

    │       it no longer equitable to apply it

    ├── (6) Any other reason that justifies

    │       relief (catch-all, requires

    │       extraordinary circumstances)

         ↓

    Standard: Extraordinary circumstances.

              Rule 60(b)(6) is a "last resort."

APPEAL PROCESS

         ↓

# **VII. CLAIM AND ISSUE PRECLUSION** {#vii.-claim-and-issue-preclusion}

---

### **CLAIM PRECLUSION (Res Judicata)** {#claim-preclusion-(res-judicata)}

CLAIM PRECLUSION (Res Judicata)

         ↓

    "A final judgment on the merits

    bars a party from suing again

    on the SAME claim."

         ↓

    Requirements:

    ├── 1\. FINAL JUDGMENT on the MERITS

    │   → Rule 41(b) dismissals are usually

    │     on the merits. Voluntary 2nd

    │     dismissal is on the merits.

    │

    ├── 2\. SAME CLAIM

    │   → "Transactional Test": Claims are

    │     the same if they arise from the

    │     same transaction or series of

    │     connected transactions.

    │

    ├── 3\. SAME PARTIES (or their privies)

    │   → Parties who were parties to the

    │     first action, or those in legal

    │     privity with them.

         ↓

    EFFECT: Bars RE-LITIGATION of the claim.

            Prevents bringing claims that

            WERE or COULD HAVE BEEN litigated.

---

### **ISSUE PRECLUSION (Collateral Estoppel)** {#issue-preclusion-(collateral-estoppel)}

ISSUE PRECLUSION (Collateral Estoppel)

         ↓

    "Once a court decides an issue of fact

    or law necessary to its judgment, that

    decision precludes re-litigation of

    that issue in a subsequent action

    between the parties."

         ↓

    Requirements:

    ├── 1\. SAME ISSUE OF FACT OR LAW

    │   → Issue must be identical to one

    │     decided in prior action.

    │

    ├── 2\. ACTUALLY LITIGATED AND DECIDED

    │   → Issue must have been genuinely

    │     contested and decided by the court.

    │

    ├── 3\. NECESSARY TO THE JUDGMENT

    │   → The decision on the issue must

    │     have been essential to the prior

    │     judgment.

    │

    ├── 4\. FINAL JUDGMENT on the merits

    │

    └── 5\. Party against whom preclusion

            is sought was a PARTY (or privy)

            to the prior action (mutuality

            is not always required for

            non-mutual offensive collateral

            estoppel, but is generally

            required for defensive).

         ↓

    EFFECT: Bars RE-LITIGATION of the specific

            ISSUE. Does NOT bar entire claims,

            only specific issues decided.

# **Meeting Notes Outline: Rule 50, Summary Judgment & Related Doctrines** {#meeting-notes-outline:-rule-50,-summary-judgment-&-related-doctrines}

## **I. TERMINOLOGY: DIRECTED VERDICT vs. JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW** {#i.-terminology:-directed-verdict-vs.-judgment-as-a-matter-of-law}

| Stage | Old Term | Modern Federal Term (Post-1991) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Before verdict | Directed Verdict | JMOL – Rule 50(a) |
| After verdict | JNOV | Renewed JMOL – Rule 50(b) |

---

## **II. SUMMARY JUDGMENT – RULE 56** {#ii.-summary-judgment-–-rule-56}

**Rule 56(a) Standard — Two Requirements:**

1. No genuine dispute as to a material fact  
2. Movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law

**Rule 56(c) Procedures:**

* **56(c)(1):** Must support assertions by citing record evidence (depositions, affidavits, ESI, admissions, etc.) OR showing the opposing party cannot produce admissible evidence  
* **56(c)(2):** Party may object if cited material cannot be presented in admissible form  
* **56(c)(4):** Affidavits must be based on personal knowledge, contain admissible facts, and show competency of affiant

---

## **III. SEVENTH AMENDMENT – RIGHT TO JURY TRIAL** {#iii.-seventh-amendment-–-right-to-jury-trial}

**Two Clauses:**

* Right to Jury Trial Clause  
* Reexamination Clause

**Key Limitations:**

1. Does not apply to criminal cases (covered by 6th Amendment)  
2. Only applies to "suits at common law" — not equity or admiralty  
3. Federal courts only (not incorporated to states)  
4. Waivable — must request jury trial  
5. "Preserves" right as it existed in **English courts in 1791**

---

## **IV. COMMON LAW vs. EQUITY** {#iv.-common-law-vs.-equity}

| Law Courts | Equity Courts |
| ----- | ----- |
| Assumpsit, Trespass, Ejectment, Trover, Mandamus, Habeas Corpus | Injunctions, Specific Performance, Accounting, Class Actions, Interpleader |
| Sought money damages → **Jury trial** | No adequate remedy at law → **Judge decides** |
| Rigid, formal, limited remedies | Flexible procedure and remedies |

**Key Rule:** If a party seeks money damages in federal court → entitled to a jury.

---

## **V. DAIRY QUEEN, INC. v. WOOD** {#v.-dairy-queen,-inc.-v.-wood}

**Facts:** DQ licensed trademark, stopped payments (+$60K default), continued using trademark → sued for injunction \+ accounting.

**Issue:** Was DQ entitled to a jury trial?

**Holdings:**

* "Incidental legal issues" argument → **Rejected**  
* "Purely equitable" framing → **Rejected** — labels don't control jury rights  
* A contract debt claim is **undeniably legal**, regardless of how it's pleaded

**Key Principles:**

| Principle | Rule |
| ----- | ----- |
| Labels don't matter | Calling damages an "accounting" doesn't make it equitable |
| Legal issues can't be "incidental" | Judge cannot deny jury trial by labeling legal claims as secondary |
| Legal claims go first | Jury decides legal claims first; judge is bound by jury findings on common issues |
| Merger expanded jury rights | Rule 2 merged law/equity but Rule 38(a) preserves jury trial |

## **VI. RULE 50 — JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW (JMOL)** {#vi.-rule-50-—-judgment-as-a-matter-of-law-(jmol)}

### **Core Standard** {#core-standard}

A rational factfinder **could not** find for the party on the evidence presented.

### **Evidence Spectrum** {#evidence-spectrum}

| Zone | Result |
| ----- | ----- |
| Too little evidence | JMOL granted **against** burden-bearer |
| Reasonable dispute | **Jury decides** |
| Overwhelming evidence | JMOL granted **for** burden-bearer |

---

### **Rule 50(a) — Before Jury Deliberates** {#rule-50(a)-—-before-jury-deliberates}

* Motion can be made **any time before case is submitted to jury**  
* Must **specify the issue, law, and facts**  
* Court must:  
  * Draw all rational inferences for non-moving party  
  * NOT weigh evidence or make credibility determinations  
* Evidence considered: all of non-movant's evidence \+ movant's uncontradicted/unimpeached evidence

**When motions may be made:**

1. After opposing party fully heard on an issue  
2. After plaintiff rests  
3. After defendant rests

---

### **Rule 50(b) — After Verdict (Renewed JMOL)** {#rule-50(b)-—-after-verdict-(renewed-jmol)}

* Must be filed **within 28 days** of entry of judgment  
* Must be **same issue** as prior 50(a) motion  
* **Cannot make 50(b) without prior 50(a)** — reasons:  
  * Gives opposing party chance to correct deficiency  
  * Respects 7th Amendment Reexamination Clause  
  * Prevents sandbagging  
* If 50(b) is granted on appeal → jury verdict can simply be **reinstated**

---

### **Key Rules — IMPORTANT** {#key-rules-—-important}

* **50(a) must be made before case goes to jury**  
* **50(b) must mirror 50(a) issue and filed within 28 days**  
* **Cannot appeal denial of 50(a) without first filing 50(b)**  
* **Standard of review on appeal: De Novo** (same as Summary Judgment)  
* Multiple 50(a) motions are permitted

---

## **VII. PENNSYLVANIA RR v. CHAMBERLAIN** {#vii.-pennsylvania-rr-v.-chamberlain}

* Witness testimony deemed "incredible" and "unbelievable"  
* Positive, uncontradicted testimony of opposing witnesses controlled  
* Illustrates: **Incredible testimony alone cannot create a genuine factual dispute**

### **Rule 33 — Interrogatories** {#rule-33-—-interrogatories}

* Must respond in writing, **under oath**

* **No boilerplate objections**
